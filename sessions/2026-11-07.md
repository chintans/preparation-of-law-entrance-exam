## Lesson — The anti-defection law in four tests (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** You are 0/3 on GK-POL. CLAT likes the Tenth Schedule because it is in the news every year (Maharashtra 2022–23, the
Sena merger case listed for 8 Dec, Telangana 2025–26) and because its rules are numerical and easy to test with a fact pattern.

**The law in one table (Tenth Schedule, added by the 52nd Amendment, 1985):**

| Test | Rule | Number to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Who is caught? | **Para 2(1)(a)** voluntarily giving up membership of the party, which can be inferred from conduct. **Para 2(1)(b)** voting or abstaining against the party's direction without prior permission | not condoned within **15 days** |
| 2. Independents & nominated | An independent who **joins any party** is out. A nominated member may join a party within **6 months** of taking the seat, not after | 6 months |
| 3. The only escape | **Para 4 merger**: the original party merges *and* at least **two-thirds** of the legislature party agree. The old **para 3 "split" (one-third)** defence was **deleted by the 91st Amendment, 2003**, which also capped ministries at **15%** of the House (Arts. 75(1A), 164(1A)) | ≥ 2/3; 1/3 is dead |
| 4. Who decides? | **Para 6**: the Speaker/Chairman. *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992): the Speaker acts as a **tribunal**, so the decision is subject to **judicial review**. **Para 7**, which barred courts, was struck down. *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020): decide within **3 months** absent exceptional circumstances. *Subhash Desai* (2023): the **political party**, not the legislature party, appoints the whip; whether a Speaker facing a removal notice can decide (*Nabam Rebia*, 2016) is referred to 7 judges | 3 months |

**Rule of thumb.** Run the four tests in order: Caught? Exempt? Escaped by a valid 2/3 merger? Who decides, and can a court review it?
Most CLAT options fail at one identifiable step.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** Party P has 9 MPs in the Lok Sabha. Six join party Q and claim a merger. The Speaker accepts it. Which
is correct? (A) Protected: a majority left. (B) Protected: more than one-third left. (C) Protected if the merger satisfies para 4,
since 6 is two-thirds of 9; the Speaker's decision remains open to judicial review. (D) Not protected: mergers were abolished in 2003.
→ **(C).** (A) uses the wrong threshold. (B) is the deleted split rule. (D) confuses the deleted *split* with the surviving *merger*.
This is the real Shiv Sena (UBT) Lok Sabha dispute; whether a merger of the *original party* also happened is the contested point.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **Split vs merger.** One-third is the deleted 2003 split. Two-thirds is the surviving merger rule.
2. **Speaker's word as final.** It was made final by para 6, but *Kihoto* subjects it to judicial review. Para 7 is the part that fell.
3. **"Voluntarily gives up" needs a resignation.** It does not; it can be inferred from conduct.
4. **Delay vs outcome.** A court can order a Speaker to decide (and punish delay), but it reviews the decision; it does not usually
   make it in the first instance.

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 388 words**

In the Telangana Assembly election of December 2023, ten candidates won on the ticket of the Bharat Rashtra Samithi. Within months all ten had been seen at Congress events, and one of them even contested the 2024 Lok Sabha election as a Congress candidate. The BRS asked the Speaker to disqualify them under the Tenth Schedule to the Constitution, the anti-defection law added in 1985. Nothing happened for over a year.

The Tenth Schedule gives a member two main ways to lose a seat. He may "voluntarily give up" membership of the party that set him up, which courts have long said can be inferred from conduct and need not be a formal resignation. Or he may vote, or abstain, contrary to his party's direction without its prior permission, unless the party condones the act within fifteen days. Until 2003 a member could escape by showing that a third of his legislature party had split away with him; that defence was removed, leaving only a merger approved by at least two-thirds of the legislature party.

The petitions go to the Speaker. But the Supreme Court has held that, in deciding them, the Speaker acts as a tribunal, and a tribunal's decision can be examined by the courts. In July 2025 the Court found the Speaker's delay indefensible and directed him to decide all ten petitions within three months, warning that a member who stalled the hearing would face an adverse inference. When that deadline passed, the Court issued a contempt notice in November 2025, calling the inaction the grossest kind of contempt.

The Speaker then moved. Between December 2025 and March 2026 he dismissed every petition, holding that the material placed before him did not prove that any member had given up his party. A day after the last two dismissals, the Supreme Court closed the contempt case. Its complaint had been the delay, not the outcome, and the decisions were now on record.

That is not the end. The BRS and a BJP legislator have challenged the dismissals in the Telangana High Court, arguing that contesting an election on a rival party's symbol is as clear a renunciation as there can be. The case shows a familiar pattern: a court can make a Speaker decide, and can review what he decides, but the first decision remains his.
