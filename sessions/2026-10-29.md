# Day 10 — Principle application at AILET speed (AILET)

## Lesson

**Why this, today.** AILET gives you 150 questions in 120 minutes — **48 seconds each** — and its legal reasoning
sits inside the 70-question Logical Reasoning section as short, standalone principle–fact items. You already apply
principles accurately (9/9 on Days 1–2). What AILET tests on top of that is **speed without slipping on the one
qualifier**. The principles are short; the trap is almost always a single word: *in the course of*, *in good faith*,
*without his knowledge*, *unless*.

### The method — "Who · Trigger · Unless" (read the principle in ~15 seconds)
1. **Who** does the principle protect or make liable? (employer, accused, occupier…)
2. **Trigger** — the element(s) that must be present. Underline them mentally; there are usually one or two.
3. **Unless** — the exception or qualifier. This is where the answer turns most of the time.
Then read the facts asking only: *is the trigger met, and does the unless apply?* Ignore everything else —
fairness, what the real law is, who is sympathetic.

### Four principle families AILET likes (all new to you here)
- **Vicarious liability:** an employer answers for an employee's tort committed *in the course of employment* — which
  includes doing an authorised job in an unauthorised or forbidden *way*. It does **not** cover a "frolic of his own":
  an act unconnected with the job.
- **Mistake of fact (BNS ss. 14, 17; old IPC ss. 76, 79):** no offence if a person, by a mistake of **fact** — not of
  **law** — **in good faith** believes himself bound or justified by law. "Good faith" needs **due care and attention**
  (BNS s. 2(11)). Two traps: a mistake about what the law says is no defence; a careless belief is not good faith.
- **Strict liability (Rylands v Fletcher, 1868):** one who keeps on his land something likely to do mischief if it
  escapes is liable for the damage its escape causes, **even without negligence** — subject to exceptions such as
  act of God and the plaintiff's own fault. (In India, *M.C. Mehta v Union of India* (1987) went further for hazardous
  industries — "absolute" liability with no exceptions — but on AILET you apply **the principle given**.)
- **Intoxication (BNS s. 22; old IPC s. 85):** incapacity to know the nature of the act excuses **only if** the
  intoxicant was given **without his knowledge or against his will**. Voluntary drinking is no excuse.

### Worked example (AILET style — time yourself: 45 s)
*Principle:* An employer is liable for a wrong committed by an employee in the course of employment, including an
authorised act done in an unauthorised manner, but not for an act done by the employee for his own purposes.
*Facts:* A bank tells its cashier never to leave the counter. A customer argues about a deposit; the cashier leaves
the counter, follows the customer to the car park and punches him over a personal insult.
(A) The bank is liable — the quarrel began at the counter. (B) The bank is liable — the cashier disobeyed an
instruction during working hours. (C) The bank is not liable — the punch was the cashier's own act of revenge,
not a way of doing his job. (D) The bank is not liable — banks are never liable for violence by staff.
**Answer: (C).** Working hours and workplace are not the test; the question is whether the act was a *way of doing
the job*. Revenge for a personal insult is not. (D) is an absolute statement — almost always wrong in AILET.

**The trap examiners set.** Options that are *true statements of fact* ("he disobeyed instructions", "she chose to go
to the party") but not the deciding element of the principle. Train yourself to ask, for every option: *does this
reason track the trigger or the unless?* If not, cut it.

## Test — 8 questions, AILET pattern (+1 / −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted). Aim ≤ 6½ minutes (48 s each).

Apply only the principle given, even if you believe the real law differs.

**1.** *Principle:* An employer is liable for a wrong done by an employee in the course of employment, including an
authorised act done in an unauthorised manner; but not for an act done by the employee on a frolic of his own.
*Facts:* A food-delivery company expressly forbids its riders from jumping red lights. Running late with an order,
rider Vikas jumps a red light and knocks down Neha. Neha sues the company.
- (A) The company is not liable, because it had expressly forbidden jumping red lights.
- (B) The company is liable, because Vikas was delivering an order — his authorised job — in a forbidden manner.
- (C) The company is not liable, because the wrong was Vikas's personal fault.
- (D) The company is liable, because every employer is answerable for every act of its employees.

**2.** *Principle:* Same as Q1.
*Facts:* After his shift ends, Vikas, without permission, takes the company's delivery scooter to drive to his
cousin's wedding in another town and on the way hits Imran. Imran sues the company.
- (A) The company is liable, because the scooter belonged to the company.
- (B) The company is liable, because it should have secured its scooters after shifts.
- (C) The company is not liable, because Vikas's licence was in his own name.
- (D) The company is not liable, because the trip was Vikas's own errand, unconnected with his work.

**3.** *Principle:* Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, by reason of a mistake of fact and not by
reason of a mistake of law, in good faith believes himself to be justified by law in doing it. Nothing is said to be
done in good faith which is done without due care and attention.
*Facts:* A state bans the sale of single-use plastic bags. Ramesh, a shopkeeper, keeps selling them because he
honestly believes the ban applies only to big supermarkets, not small shops. He is prosecuted.
- (A) Ramesh has no defence, because his mistake was about what the law says — a mistake of law.
- (B) Ramesh has a defence, because he honestly believed he was allowed to sell the bags.
- (C) Ramesh has a defence, because a small shopkeeper cannot be expected to read every notification.
- (D) Ramesh has no defence, because he did not take due care about the facts of his own shop.

**4.** *Principle:* Same as Q3.
*Facts:* A mall security guard is told by the store manager that a shoplifter is in a red jacket. He checks the
store's CCTV, which shows the shoplifter's face, and then detains a man in a red jacket with the same face near the
exit. The man turns out to be the shoplifter's identical twin. The twin complains of wrongful restraint.
- (A) The guard is guilty, because he detained an innocent man.
- (B) The guard is guilty, because only the police may detain a suspect.
- (C) The guard has a defence, because he acted on a mistake of fact in good faith after checking the CCTV.
- (D) The guard has a defence, because the store manager told him to act.

**5.** *Principle:* A person who brings and keeps on his land anything likely to do mischief if it escapes is liable
for the damage caused by its escape, even if he took all reasonable care; but he is not liable where the escape is
caused by an act of God or by the plaintiff's own fault.
*Facts:* A dye factory stores chemical effluent in a large tank. Despite regular inspections, a valve fails without
warning and the effluent flows into Harpreet's adjoining field, destroying her wheat. The factory proves it took
every reasonable precaution.
- (A) The factory is not liable, because it was not negligent.
- (B) The factory is not liable, because a sudden valve failure is an act of God.
- (C) The factory is liable, because the effluent escaped and caused damage, and care is no defence under the principle.
- (D) The factory is liable only if Harpreet proves which employee was careless.

**6.** *Principle:* Same as Q5.
*Facts:* Harpreet's farmhand, on her instructions, secretly drills a hole in the factory's tank at night to take
effluent to use as cheap fertiliser. The effluent pours out through the hole and ruins her own wheat. She sues the
factory.
- (A) The factory is not liable, because the escape was caused by Harpreet's own fault.
- (B) The factory is liable, because it kept a dangerous substance on its land.
- (C) The factory is liable, because it should have guarded the tank at night.
- (D) The factory is not liable, because the farmhand, not Harpreet, drilled the hole.

**7.** *Principle:* Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, is by reason of
intoxication incapable of knowing the nature of the act, provided that the thing which intoxicated him was
administered to him without his knowledge or against his will.
*Facts:* At an office party Sanjay drinks only juice. As a prank, a colleague secretly mixes a heavy sedative-laced
spirit into it. Sanjay, unable to understand what he is doing, picks up a guest's phone from a table and walks out
with it. He is charged with theft.
- (A) Sanjay is guilty, because he chose to attend a party where alcohol was served.
- (B) Sanjay is guilty, because he physically took the phone.
- (C) Sanjay has a defence only if he returns the phone.
- (D) Sanjay has a defence, because the intoxicant was given to him without his knowledge and he could not know the nature of his act.

**8.** *Principle:* An agreement with a minor is void from the beginning. It cannot be enforced against him, either
while he is a minor or after he attains majority; a promise made after majority to honour it binds him only if it is
a fresh contract supported by fresh consideration.
*Facts:* At 17, Kunal agrees to buy a used motorbike from Dev for ₹60,000, pays nothing and takes the bike. A month
after turning 18, Kunal signs a note: "I confirm my earlier purchase and will pay ₹60,000 by June." In return, Dev
agrees to transfer the bike's registration into Kunal's name, which he had until then refused to do. Kunal does not
pay. Dev sues on the note.
- (A) Dev fails, because a void agreement can never be confirmed after majority.
- (B) Dev succeeds, because the note is a fresh promise by a major, supported by fresh consideration — Dev's promise to transfer the registration.
- (C) Dev succeeds, because Kunal kept the bike and so the original agreement became valid.
- (D) Dev fails, because a motorbike is not a necessary.
