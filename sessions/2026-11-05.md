### Language — Q1–4

**Passage (Q1–2) — 227 words.** *(Optional: time your read and send `R m:ss`. Level 1 target is 1:16 at 180 wpm.)*

Cities have always been warmer than the countryside around them, but the gap is widening. Concrete and asphalt soak up sunlight through the day and release it slowly at night, so that a city street can stay several degrees hotter than a nearby field long after sunset. Planners call this the urban heat island, and for the poorest residents it is not an abstraction: those who work outdoors, or sleep under tin roofs, bear its cost first.

The remedies most often proposed are expensive: reflective paint, district cooling, new building codes. Yet the cheapest remedy is also the oldest. A mature tree shades the ground beneath it and, by drawing water from the soil and releasing it through its leaves, cools the air around it as well. Studies of tree-lined streets repeatedly find lower surface temperatures than on bare ones.

The difficulty is that trees work slowly and are easy to lose. A sapling planted for a photograph seldom survives without years of watering and protection, while a road-widening scheme can remove in a week what took forty years to grow. Planting drives therefore tell us little; what matters is how many trees are still standing a decade later. A city serious about heat would count survival, not saplings, and would treat an old tree less as an obstacle to development than as infrastructure it already owns.
