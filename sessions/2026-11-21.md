## Constitution Capsule — Unit 8 again: Articles 20–22 (≈ 4 min, before the clock)

You did not get to this on Thursday, so it runs once more; the questions below come from earlier units.

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 20(1)** → no *ex post facto* **criminal** law: no conviction for an act that was not an offence when done; no heavier penalty than the one then in force.
   → Civil and tax laws *can* be retrospective; Art 20(1) is about crimes only.
2. **Art 20(2)** → no one **prosecuted and punished** twice for the same offence. → Both words needed; a departmental inquiry is not a prosecution (*Maqbool Hussain*, 1953).
3. **Art 20(3)** → an **accused** cannot be compelled to be a witness against himself. → Fingerprints and handwriting samples are not "testimony" (*Kathi Kalu Oghad*, 1961,
   11 judges); forced narco or polygraph tests are (*Selvi*, 2010).
4. **Art 22(1)–(2)** → arrested person: grounds told, a lawyer of choice, **before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours** (journey time excluded). → Not for enemy aliens or
   preventive detainees (22(3)).
5. **Art 22(4)–(7)** → preventive detention beyond **3 months** needs an **Advisory Board** report; grounds must be given (22(5)), facts may be withheld in the public
   interest (22(6)).

**Say-it drill** — cover the lines and say each: **20(1) · 20(2) · 20(3) · 22(2) · 22(4)**.

**Case:** ***D. K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997)** — the Supreme Court laid down binding arrest guidelines (identification of the arresting officer, an arrest memo
attested by a witness, informing a relative, medical examination); most now sit in the criminal procedure code (BNSS **ss. 36 and 48**; old CrPC ss. 41B and 50A).

**The trap:** Art 20 protects **every person** (citizens, foreigners, companies), and Arts 20 and 21 **cannot be suspended in an emergency** (Art 359). Art 22's
24-hour rule **excludes travel time** — "24 hours including the journey" is the wrong option.

### Section A — English (Questions 1–7)

**⏱ Passage (Questions 1–4) — 355 words · target 1:58 (level 1, 180 wpm) · technique: no regression.** Glance at the four stems (~15 s), then time the read
only — pen moving steadily under the lines, no going back — and note `R m:ss`.

When a new technology unsettles an old market, governments face a choice that looks simpler than it is: ban the newcomer or write rules for it. Prohibition has an obvious appeal. It is quick, it is cheap to announce, and it reassures the incumbents who complain the loudest. A ban also spares the regulator the hard work of deciding what safe operation would actually look like.

Yet prohibition rarely makes a demand disappear. When commuters want a cheap ride through traffic, or households want to rent out a spare room, a ban tends to push the activity out of sight rather than out of existence. The service carries on, now without insurance, inspection or any record of who is responsible when something goes wrong. The state gives up the very information it would need to protect the people it claims to be protecting.

Regulation asks more of the state. It must identify the real risks — an untrained rider, an unsafe vehicle, a passenger with no remedy — and design a rule aimed at each. Done well, this produces a market that is both larger and safer than either the unregulated free-for-all or the black market that a ban creates. Done badly, it produces paperwork that only large firms can afford, and so entrenches the very companies whose dominance worried regulators in the first place.

None of this means that bans are never justified. Where an activity cannot be made safe at any reasonable cost, or where the harm falls on people who never chose to bear it, prohibition may be the honest answer. The point is narrower: a ban should be the conclusion of an inquiry into risk, not a substitute for one. A government that reaches for prohibition first is often announcing not that the activity is dangerous, but that regulating it seemed like too much trouble.

Courts, too, have begun to ask this question when a ban restricts the freedom to carry on a trade. They want to know not only whether a restriction pursues a legitimate aim, but whether a less drastic measure would have served that aim just as well.

**Principle for Questions 17–19.** Every person has a right to defend his own body, or the body of any other person, against any offence affecting the human
body. The right does not extend to causing more harm than is necessary for the purpose of defence. It begins as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger
to the body arises and lasts only as long as that apprehension continues. There is no right of private defence where there is time to have recourse to the
protection of the public authorities.
