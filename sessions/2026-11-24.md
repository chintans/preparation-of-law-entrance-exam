## Constitution Capsule — Unit 10: Articles 23–30 (≈ 4 min, before the clock)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 23** → bans **traffic in human beings, begar and forced labour**, and it applies against private persons too. → Paying less than the minimum wage can amount to "forced
   labour" (*PUDR / Asiad Workers*, 1982). The State may still impose **compulsory service for public purposes** (23(2)), without discrimination by religion, race, caste or class.
2. **Art 24** → no child **below 14** in a **factory, mine or other hazardous employment**. → The age is 14, not 18 (*M. C. Mehta v. State of T.N.*, 1996: child-labour
   rehabilitation fund).
3. **Art 25** → **all persons** have freedom of conscience and the right to **profess, practise and propagate** religion, subject to **public order, morality, health** and the other
   Fundamental Rights. → 25(2) lets the State regulate **secular** activity tied to religion and throw open Hindu temples to all. "Propagate" does **not** include a right to convert
   another person (*Rev. Stainislaus*, 1977).
4. **Arts 26–28** → **26**: a religious denomination manages its own affairs in matters of religion. **27**: no tax whose proceeds go to promoting a particular religion. **28**: no
   religious instruction in institutions **wholly maintained by State funds**.
5. **Arts 29–30** → **29(1)**: **any section of citizens** with a distinct language, script or culture may conserve it. **30(1)**: **minorities** (religious or linguistic) may establish and
   administer educational institutions of their choice. → Minority status is decided **State-wise** (*T. M. A. Pai*, 2002, 11 judges).

**Say-it drill** — cover the lines and say each: **23 · 24 · 25 · 26 · 30**.

**Case:** ***Commissioner, HR&CE v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt* (1954, 7 judges)** started the **essential religious practices** test: "religion" in Arts 25–26
covers rituals and observances that are integral to it, and the State may regulate only what is secular. The Court used the same test in *Shayara Bano* (2017, 5 judges, 3:2) and
held that instant triple talaq was not essential to Islam.

**The trap:** **Art 25** protects *all persons*, but **Art 29** protects *any section of citizens*, and it is not limited to minorities. **Art 30** is for *minorities only*. An option that
says "Art 29 protects only minorities" or "Art 24 bars work below 18" is wrong.

### Section A — English (Questions 1–4)

**⏱ Passage (Questions 1–4) — 408 words · target 2:16 (level 1, 180 wpm) · technique: chunking.** Glance at the four stems (~15 s), then time the read only. Take the

India runs on one clock. Indian Standard Time, set to the meridian at 82.5° East near Mirzapur, is five and a half hours ahead of Greenwich, and it governs everything from train timetables to the closing hours of banks in Kohima and in Kutch. Yet the sun does not obey the timetable. At the eastern edge of the country it rises nearly two hours before it does at the western edge, so that a winter afternoon in Arunachal Pradesh is already dark by four while offices there stay open until five.

Advocates of a second time zone argue that this mismatch has costs that never appear on a balance sheet. Workers in the North-East, they say, wake in daylight and spend it travelling, then switch on lights for the hours that a sensible clock would have given back to them. Studies of electricity use suggest that moving the eastern clock forward by an hour could trim evening peak demand, and tea gardens in Assam have long kept their own "bagan time" for precisely this reason.

The objections are less about astronomy than about administration. A country with one clock has one railway timetable, one set of broadcast schedules and one opening bell for its markets. Two clocks would create a boundary somewhere, and every train, flight and bank transfer crossing it would need converting. Critics warn of missed connections and confused shift rosters; some add, less convincingly, that a separate time for one region would weaken the sense of a single nation.

Neither side is entirely right. The fear of confusion is real but exaggerated: countries far larger than India manage several zones, and software now does most of the converting that once fell to clerks. The promised savings, on the other hand, depend on behaviour. A clock that moves an hour forward saves electricity only if offices and schools actually change their hours with it; a region that keeps its old habits under a new name gains nothing.

What the debate needs, then, is less rhetoric and more evidence. A pilot — an earlier working day for government offices in the North-East, without touching the official clock — would show whether people use the daylight they are given. If they do, the case for a second zone becomes an argument about convenience; if they do not, it was never really about the clock at all.

### Section C — Legal Reasoning (Questions 10–14)

**Passage 1 (Questions 10–12) — 142 words.**

A person commits theft if, intending to take any movable property dishonestly out of the possession of another without that other's consent, he moves the property in order to
take it. A person acts "dishonestly" when he intends to cause wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another. Wrongful loss includes being wrongfully kept out of one's
property, even for a time; the taker need not intend to keep it forever. A person who takes property under an honest claim of right, believing in good faith that it is his own,
does not act dishonestly, even if the belief later turns out to be mistaken. And because theft is an offence against *possession*, an owner can commit theft of his own property
if he dishonestly takes it from someone who holds it lawfully, for instance as security for a loan.

**10.** Ravi pledges his gold watch with a jeweller, Yusuf, as security for a loan of ₹10,000. Before repaying anything, Ravi notices the watch on Yusuf's counter while Yusuf is
busy, slips it into his pocket and leaves. Has Ravi committed theft?
- (A) No, because a person cannot commit theft of his own watch.
- (B) Yes, because he dishonestly moved the watch out of Yusuf's lawful possession without Yusuf's consent.
- (C) No, because he still intends to repay the loan some day.
- (D) Yes, but only if he never repays the loan.

**11.** Revenue records show a field in Meena's name, and she honestly believes it is hers. She harvests the standing wheat and carries it home. A civil court later rules that the
field, and so the crop, belonged to her neighbour Suresh. Has Meena committed theft?
- (A) Yes, because she moved the wheat out of Suresh's possession without his consent.
- (B) Yes, because ignorance of the law is no excuse.
- (C) No, because wheat harvested from a field is not movable property.
- (D) No, because she took the wheat under an honest claim of right and so did not act dishonestly.

**12.** Tanvi takes her colleague Uday's laptop from his desk without asking and hides it in a cupboard for a week so that he misses a project deadline. She then returns it
undamaged. Has Tanvi committed theft?
- (A) Yes, because she intended to keep Uday wrongfully out of his property, which is wrongful loss even though she meant to return it.
- (B) No, because she always intended to return the laptop.
- (C) No, because she gained nothing from taking the laptop.
- (D) No, because hiding something in the same office is not "moving" it.

**Passage 2 (Questions 13–14) — 92 words.**

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and the equal protection of the laws. It forbids class legislation but permits reasonable classification. A classification is
valid only if it meets **both** of two tests: (i) it rests on an *intelligible differentia* that separates those grouped together from those left out, and (ii) the differentia
has a *rational nexus* with the object the law seeks to achieve. Article 14 does not demand mathematical precision; a line drawn somewhere sensible is not invalid just because
someone falls just on the wrong side of it.

**13.** To reduce air pollution, a State imposes a new "green levy" on motor vehicles. The levy applies only to vehicles **less than five years old**. Older vehicles, which
emit more, are exempt. A new-car owner challenges the levy under Article 14. The most likely outcome is that the levy is:
- (A) valid, because vehicle age is an intelligible differentia
- (B) valid, because the State has wide freedom in fiscal matters and Article 14 does not apply to taxes
- (C) invalid, because although age is an intelligible differentia, taxing only newer vehicles has no rational nexus with reducing pollution
- (D) invalid, because every classification between vehicles violates Article 14

**14.** A city bans street vending within 100 metres of hospital gates to keep ambulance routes clear. A vendor 95 metres from the gate argues that the rule is
discriminatory because vendors at 105 metres may carry on. The vendor's challenge will most likely:
- (A) succeed, because vendors at 95 and 105 metres are in identical positions
- (B) succeed, because any restriction on trade violates Article 14
- (C) fail, because street vendors have no fundamental rights
- (D) fail, because distance from the gate is an intelligible differentia linked to keeping ambulance access clear, and the line need not be mathematically perfect
