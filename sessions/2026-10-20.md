# Day 1 — Principle-to-fact application (CLAT Legal Reasoning)

## Lesson

**The concept.** Every CLAT legal question gives you a *principle* and a set of *facts*. Your job is
not to know the law — it is to apply the principle exactly as written, even if real law says
something different. The examiner is testing discipline, not knowledge. Legal Reasoning is ~25% of
CLAT (28–32 questions), and the same skill drives AILET reasoning and NLSAT Part A and B.

**The method — "Elements, Deciding fact, Eliminate" (EDE):**
1. **Read the question stem first.** Know whether you are asked "is X liable", "which fact is most
   relevant", or "which option best weakens".
2. **Break the principle into elements** — a checklist. Watch for words that add or remove an
   element: *and*, *only*, *unless*, *provided that*, *except*.
3. **Map facts onto each element** and find the **deciding fact** — the one fact that makes an element
   pass or fail. Facts that touch no element are decoration (and often bait).
4. **Eliminate options.** Strike any option that (a) relies on law not in the passage, (b) reaches the
   right conclusion for a wrong reason, (c) uses absolute words ("never", "always") the principle
   does not support.

**Worked example (CLAT style).**
*Principle:* An acceptance must be communicated to the offeror. Mere mental assent, not expressed by
word or conduct, is not acceptance.
*Facts:* Asha emails Bilal offering her used car for ₹3 lakh: "If I don't hear from you by Friday, I'll
take it that you've agreed." Bilal decides to buy, tells his wife, and withdraws cash, but sends no
reply. On Saturday Asha sells the car to Charu. Bilal sues Asha.
*Options:* (A) Bilal wins: Asha herself fixed silence as acceptance. (B) Bilal loses: he never paid the
price. (C) Bilal loses: his decision was never communicated to Asha. (D) Bilal wins: withdrawing cash
was acceptance by conduct.
*Working:* Elements → (i) acceptance (ii) communicated. Deciding fact: *no reply was sent*; telling his
wife and withdrawing cash were never communicated to Asha. (A) is the emotional trap — the offeror
cannot impose silence as acceptance under this principle. (B) is right conclusion, wrong reason
(payment is not an element). (D) fails because conduct must reach the offeror. **Answer: C.**

**The traps examiners set.**
- *Real-law pull:* an option that is true in actual law but not stated in the passage.
- *Right result, wrong reason:* CLAT options almost always come as "X wins/loses, because …". Grade
  the *because*.
- *The exception clause:* one line ("however", "this does not apply where …") flips half the questions.
- *Sympathy bait:* the morally attractive party is not always the legally correct one.

**Context:** CLAT increasingly sets criminal-law passages using the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
(BNS), which replaced the IPC from 1 July 2024 — so expect BNS section numbers (e.g., theft is now
s. 303) in passages. The principle in the passage still governs.

## Test — 8 questions, ~10 minutes, +1 / −0.25

### Passage I (Q1–4)

A person who freely and voluntarily agrees to run the risk of a particular harm cannot later claim
compensation for that harm from the one who caused it. This defence, long known by the maxim
*volenti non fit injuria* ("to one who is willing, no wrong is done"), requires two things. First, the
claimant must have known of the risk. Second, the claimant must have agreed, expressly or through
conduct, to bear it. Knowledge, on its own, is not agreement. A labourer who knows that the machine
she operates is dangerous, but continues because she would otherwise lose her livelihood, has not
necessarily agreed to bear that danger; agreement extracted by economic compulsion or by fraud is no
agreement at all.

The agreement also has limits in scope. A person attending a sporting event accepts the risks that
ordinarily arise from the sport itself — a ball struck into the stands at a cricket match, or a puck
deflected over the boards at a hockey game. She does not accept risks created by the organiser's own
carelessness, such as a stand that has not been maintained, or a safety measure that has been
removed. A notice at the gate stating that spectators "attend at their own risk" may show that the
spectator was told of the ordinary risks, but it cannot widen the agreement to cover the organiser's
negligence.

Finally, the defence has no application to rescuers. Where the defendant's wrongful act creates a
danger to others, and a person steps in to rescue those in danger and is hurt, the rescuer is not
treated as having agreed to the risk. The law does not wish to penalise courage that the defendant's
own wrong made necessary.

**1.** Rahul buys a ticket to a motor race. A notice at the entrance reads "Spectators attend at their
own risk." During the race, a car skids on a bend in an ordinary racing accident, and a piece of debris
flies over the regulation barrier and injures Rahul. He sues the organiser. Decide.
- (A) Rahul succeeds, as a notice can never exclude liability for injury.
- (B) Rahul fails, as he agreed to the risks ordinarily arising from watching a motor race.
- (C) Rahul succeeds, as he could not have known that this particular car would skid.
- (D) Rahul fails, as organisers of sporting events are not liable to spectators.

**2.** Same race, but the organiser had removed the barrier on that bend to fit in an extra row of
seats and sell more tickets. Rahul, seated in that row, is hit by debris. Decide.
- (A) Rahul fails, because the notice at the entrance warned him of the risk.
- (B) Rahul fails, because he chose a seat closer to the track, knowing it was closer.
- (C) Rahul succeeds, because notices at sporting venues have no legal effect.
- (D) Rahul succeeds, because the risk arose from the organiser's removal of a safety measure, which
  he did not agree to bear.

**3.** Meena works a crane at a factory. She tells her supervisor that its brake is faulty. He replies
that the repair will take a month and that she must keep working or lose her job. She continues and
is injured when the brake fails. The employer pleads *volenti non fit injuria*. Decide.
- (A) The defence fails: Meena knew of the risk, but her continuing to work under threat of losing her
  job was not free agreement to bear it.
- (B) The defence succeeds: Meena knew of the risk and chose to continue working.
- (C) The defence succeeds: her complaint proves that she fully understood the danger.
- (D) The defence fails: an employer can never rely on the agreement of an employee.

**4.** Kabir, driving rashly, hits an auto-rickshaw carrying three children, which tips into a canal.
Imran, a passer-by, jumps in, pulls one child out, and breaks his leg on the rocks. Kabir argues that
Imran saw the rocks and voluntarily took the risk. Decide.
- (A) Imran fails, because he knew of the rocks and chose to jump.
- (B) Imran fails, because he was under no legal duty to rescue the children.
- (C) Imran succeeds, because a person who is hurt rescuing others from a danger created by the
  defendant's wrong is not treated as having agreed to the risk.
- (D) Imran succeeds only for the one child he saved, not for the attempt as a whole.

### Passage II (Q5–8)

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, a person commits theft if, intending to take dishonestly
any movable property out of the possession of another person without that person's consent, he moves
that property in order to take it. A person acts "dishonestly" when he acts with the intention of
causing wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another. Wrongful loss includes being kept
out of property to which one is legally entitled, even for a time — the taker need not mean to keep
the property forever.

The offence is about *possession*, not ownership. It is therefore possible for an owner to commit
theft of his own property, if he takes it dishonestly from someone who holds it lawfully — for
example, a person to whom it has been pledged as security for a loan. Equally, the movement required
is slight: lifting an item a few centimetres in order to take it completes the act.

What the offence cannot do without is the dishonest intention. A person who takes property in the
honest belief that he is legally entitled to it does not commit theft, even if the belief turns out
to be mistaken. The honesty of the belief, not its correctness, is what matters. Whether the belief
was honestly held is judged from the circumstances: a belief that no reasonable person in the
taker's position could have held may be evidence that it was not truly held at all.

**5.** Arjun pledges his gold watch with Kamal, a jeweller, as security for a loan of ₹20,000. Before
repaying, Arjun walks into the shop while Kamal is busy, takes the watch from the counter, and leaves.
Has Arjun committed theft?
- (A) No, because a person cannot steal his own property.
- (B) Yes, because he moved the watch out of Kamal's lawful possession without consent, causing Kamal
  wrongful loss.
- (C) No, because Arjun intended to repay the loan later.
- (D) Yes, because pledging the watch made Kamal its owner.

**6.** Sunita parks her blue bicycle at the college stand. That evening she rides away on an identical
blue bicycle, believing it to be hers; it belongs to Priya. Sunita's own bicycle was parked two spaces
away. Has Sunita committed theft?
- (A) Yes, because she moved the bicycle, and the slightest movement completes the act.
- (B) Yes, because the bicycle was in fact Priya's, and Sunita's belief was mistaken.
- (C) No, because Priya's bicycle was left unattended and was therefore in no one's possession.
- (D) No, because she took it in the honest belief that it was hers, so she lacked dishonest intention.

**7.** Vikram takes his neighbour Ravi's car without asking, for a weekend trip, intending all along to
return it on Monday. He returns it on Monday with a full tank. Has Vikram committed theft?
- (A) Yes, because keeping Ravi out of his car for the weekend is wrongful loss, and the taker need not
  intend to keep the property permanently.
- (B) No, because he returned the car in a better condition than he took it.
- (C) No, because he never intended to deprive Ravi of the car permanently.
- (D) Yes, because using a car without the owner's permission is always theft.

**8.** Which of the following, if true, would most help a person accused of theft under the passage?
- (A) He returned the property within an hour of taking it.
- (B) He moved the property only a few metres before being stopped.
- (C) He honestly, though wrongly, believed that a court order entitled him to take the property.
- (D) The property belonged to him, though it was lawfully held by another as security.
