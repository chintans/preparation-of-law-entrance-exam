## Lesson — International organisations and their HQs (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** Static GK is overdue, and your last HQ question went wrong in the most common way: you knew both organisations (ISA and
IRENA) and swapped their cities. AILET's GK section asks HQs as one-liners or as "which pair is incorrectly matched". The fix is to
learn **by city, not by organisation**, and then to drill the look-alike pairs separately.

**Method — eight cities, one tag each.**
1. **Geneva — "health, work, trade, ideas, refugees":** WHO, ILO, WTO, WIPO, UNHCR, plus ITU (telecom), WMO (weather), UNCTAD, ICRC
   (Red Cross). UN's second office (Palais des Nations).
2. **Vienna — "atoms, oil, industry, drugs":** IAEA, OPEC, UNIDO, UNODC.
3. **Rome — "the food trio":** FAO, WFP, IFAD.
4. **Paris — "culture, the rich-country club, energy":** UNESCO, OECD, IEA (International Energy Agency), FATF secretariat, RSF.
5. **Nairobi — "environment and cities":** UNEP, UN-Habitat.
6. **The Hague — "courts":** ICJ (UN's court, disputes between **States**), ICC (International Criminal Court — tries **individuals**;
   set up by the **Rome** Statute 1998 but sits in The Hague), Permanent Court of Arbitration, OPCW (chemical weapons).
7. **New York / Washington DC:** UN HQ, UNICEF, UNDP, UNFPA, UN Women (New York); IMF and World Bank (Washington).
8. **Asia — the banks and blocs:** ADB **Manila** · AIIB **Beijing** · NDB (BRICS bank) **Shanghai** · ASEAN **Jakarta** · SAARC
   **Kathmandu** · BIMSTEC **Dhaka** · SCO **Beijing** · ISA **Gurugram** (Gwal Pahari) · IRENA **Abu Dhabi** (Masdar City).

Odd ones worth one line: Interpol **Lyon** · ICAO **Montreal** · IMO (maritime) **London** · UPU **Bern** · IOC **Lausanne** · FIFA
**Zurich** · ICC (cricket) **Dubai** · IUCN and WWF **Gland** (Switzerland) · UNFCCC secretariat **Bonn** · IORA **Ebene, Mauritius**.

**Worked example (AILET style).** *Which pair is correctly matched?* (A) International Solar Alliance — Abu Dhabi (B) IAEA — Paris
(C) New Development Bank — Shanghai (D) UNEP — Geneva. → **(C).** (A) is IRENA's city; (B) mixes up IAEA (Vienna) with IEA (Paris);
(D) UNEP is Nairobi — Geneva is the default guess examiners count on.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **Twin acronyms.** IEA (Paris) vs IAEA (Vienna); IMO (London) vs WMO (Geneva); ICC court (The Hague) vs ICC cricket (Dubai).
2. **Founded-at vs based-at.** ISA was launched at COP21 **Paris** (2015) but sits in Gurugram; the ICC's statute was signed in **Rome**
   but the court sits in The Hague.
3. **The two BRICS-era banks.** AIIB = Beijing; NDB = Shanghai.
4. **"Geneva" as the lazy answer.** If an option says Geneva for an environment, food or energy body, check the city tag first.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 2: Governor and assent to Bills (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 153** → a Governor for each State; since the **7th Amendment (1956)** one person can be Governor of two or more States.
2. **Arts 155–156** → appointed by the **President** by warrant under hand and seal; holds office **during the President's pleasure**;
   five-year term (156(3)) is subject to that pleasure.
3. **Art 163** → the Council of Ministers headed by the **CM** aids and advises the Governor, except where the Constitution requires him
   to act in his discretion; under **163(2)** his own decision on whether a matter is discretionary is final.
4. **Art 200** → on a Bill the Governor may **assent, withhold assent, return it** for reconsideration (**not a Money Bill** — first
   proviso), or **reserve** it for the President. If the House passes it again, he **shall not withhold** assent.
5. **Art 201** → on a reserved Bill the **President** assents or withholds; for a non-Money Bill he may direct it to be returned, and the
   House must reconsider it within **six months**.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts and say what each does: **153 · 156 · 163 · 200 · 201.** Then check.

**Case.** *B. P. Singhal v. Union of India* (2010, **5 judges**): the President's "pleasure" under Art 156 is not unfettered — a Governor
cannot be removed arbitrarily or merely for being out of step with the Union government's policies; removal is open to limited judicial
review. (Current link: the 16th Presidential Reference, 20 Nov 2025 — no court-fixed timelines under Arts 200/201 and no "deemed assent".)

**The trap.** "The Governor may return **any** Bill" — wrong: a **Money Bill can't be returned** (you have missed this twice). And the
President, not the Governor, finally decides a **reserved** Bill (Art 201).

**Passage (Questions 1–3) — 376 words**

Few cities have shaped the language of international cooperation as much as Geneva. The story begins in 1863, when a group of citizens that included the businessman Henri Dunant founded what became the International Committee of the Red Cross. A year later, diplomats meeting in the city adopted the first Geneva Convention, which promised care for wounded soldiers whichever side they fought on. The idea that war itself could have rules took its name from the city, and the four Conventions of 1949 still carry it.

After the First World War, the League of Nations chose Geneva as its seat, and the International Labour Organization, created in 1919, settled there too. The League failed to prevent another war, but it left behind buildings, habits and a pool of trained officials. When the United Nations was founded in 1945, its headquarters went to New York, yet the Palais des Nations in Geneva became its second great office. The World Health Organization arrived in 1948; the World Trade Organization, which succeeded the old GATT, followed in 1995.

Why did so many bodies cluster in one place? Neutrality is the usual answer, and it explains part of the story: a country that stayed out of both world wars looked like safe ground for rivals. But neutrality alone does not explain the pattern. Switzerland did not even join the United Nations until 2002. What mattered as much was accumulation. Each new organisation found lawyers, translators, diplomats and records already in place, and each arrival made the next one cheaper. Delegations that came for one meeting found it easy to attend three.

This clustering has a cost that is easy to miss. Poorer countries struggle to keep full missions in an expensive city, so the conversations that set global rules on health, trade and work can be dominated by those who can afford to stay. Some organisations have answered by moving staff to regional offices; others now hold more of their meetings online.

None of this has made Geneva less central. For a student of international affairs, the useful lesson is simpler: institutions grow where earlier institutions have already done the slow work of building trust, and a headquarters is often a record of history rather than a statement of function.
