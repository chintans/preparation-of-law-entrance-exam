## Lesson — CLAT Quant caselets (≈ 6 min)

**What CLAT does.** Quantitative Techniques is the smallest section, about 10–12 of 120 questions. It always comes as **one or two caselets**: a short
paragraph, sometimes with a table, and five or six questions on it. The maths is Class-10 level: ratios, percentages, averages. You lose marks in two
ways: you misread what the base is, or you spend four minutes where one would do.

**The method: TABLE → BASE → MULTIPLY**
1. **Table first (≈ 60 s).** Before reading any question, turn the paragraph into a small grid with one row per item and one column per year. Convert ratios at
   once: *total ÷ sum of parts = value of one part*.
2. **Name the base in every question.** "% change" is measured on the **old** value. A "share in 2026" is taken over the **2026 total**. "Percentage points" is a
   difference between two shares, not a % change.
3. **Multiply, don't recompute.** +20% means × 1.2 and −10% means × 0.9. For a per-unit figure, divide the multipliers: if billing goes × 1.2 and headcount goes
   × 1.2, billing per head goes × 1.0, so it is unchanged.
4. **Look at the options before you do long division.** CLAT options are usually far enough apart that one decimal place is enough.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** A High Court had 60,000 pending cases, civil : criminal : writ = 5 : 3 : 2. A year later civil cases fell 10%, criminal rose
20% and writs rose 25%.
- One part = 60,000 ÷ 10 = 6,000. So civil is 30,000, criminal 18,000 and writ 12,000.
- A year later: civil 30,000 × 0.9 = 27,000; criminal 18,000 × 1.2 = 21,600; writ 12,000 × 1.25 = 15,000. The total is 63,600.
- Overall change: 3,600 ÷ 60,000 = **+6%**. It is not the average of −10, +20 and +25, because the groups are different sizes.
- Criminal share: 18/60 = 30% before, and 21,600/63,600 ≈ 34.0% after. That is a rise of **≈ 4 percentage points**. The criminal *number* rose 20%; its *share*
  rose by only 4 points.

**The traps examiners set**
- The share of the **new** total worked out on the **old** total.
- Averaging percentage changes as if every item were the same size.
- A ratio asked "A : B" and answered "B : A". Write the ratio down in the order of the question.
- A per-unit question (per lawyer, per case) answered from the numerator alone.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 9: Article 21 (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 21** → no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty **except according to procedure established by law**. *A. K. Gopalan* (1950) read
   "law" as any enacted law, however harsh.
2. ***Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978, 7 judges)** → the procedure must be **right, just and fair**, not arbitrary. Arts 14, 19 and 21 are not watertight
   compartments; this is the "golden triangle". The case was about impounding a passport.
3. ***K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017, 9 judges, unanimous)** → **privacy** is a fundamental right under Art 21 (and Part III generally). It overruled
   *M. P. Sharma* (1954, 8 judges) and *Kharak Singh* (1962, 6 judges) on this point.
4. **Art 21A** → free and compulsory education for children aged **6–14**. It was inserted by the **86th Amendment, 2002**, which also added the duty in
   **Art 51A(k)** and rewrote Art 45 to cover children under 6. The RTE Act, 2009 has been in force since 1 Apr 2010.
5. ***Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018, 5 judges)** → the **right to die with dignity** is part of Art 21. The Court allowed passive euthanasia and living
   wills (advance directives). Active euthanasia remains unlawful.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the lines above and say what each one did: **Gopalan · Maneka · Puttaswamy · 21A · Common Cause**.

**Case:** ***Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018, 5 judges)** read down **s. 377 IPC** so that it no longer covers consensual sex between adults. The
Court relied on dignity, autonomy and privacy under Art 21 (with Arts 14, 15 and 19) and overruled *Suresh Kumar Koushal* (2013).

**The trap:** Art 21 protects **every person**, citizens and foreigners alike. Along with Art 20, it **cannot be suspended in an emergency** (Art 359, after the
44th Amendment). The text says "procedure established by law", not "due process". *Maneka* added fairness, but it did not copy the American clause. Art 21A
came with the **86th** Amendment (2002), not the 42nd.

For its first quarter-century, the Supreme Court read Article 21 as a modest promise. The Article says that no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty "except according to procedure established by law". In *A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950), the majority took "law" to mean any law validly enacted by a legislature. If the legislature prescribed a procedure, however harsh, the court's inquiry ended there: the framers, the judges said, had chosen those words precisely to avoid the American idea of "due process", under which judges could test a law against their own sense of fairness.

The narrow reading had a cost. It meant that personal liberty was only as secure as the legislature chose to make it. A statute could permit detention without reasons, or the seizure of documents without a hearing, and still satisfy Article 21 so long as it was a statute.

*Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) changed the question. Mrs Gandhi's passport had been impounded "in the interests of the general public", and the government declined to give her its reasons. A seven-judge bench held that the procedure Article 21 requires cannot be arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; it must be "right, just and fair". The fundamental rights, the Court said, are not watertight compartments. A law that deprives a person of liberty must satisfy Article 21 and, where it touches them, Articles 14 and 19 as well.

Two consequences follow. First, there must be a law. An executive instruction, however well meant, is not "law" for this purpose; the state cannot take away liberty on the strength of an order that no legislature has authorised. Second, the law's procedure must itself be fair. Ordinarily that means the person affected must be heard before the decision is taken. The Court accepted, however, that urgency may justify acting first and hearing afterwards: where notice would defeat the purpose of the action — a passport holder warned in advance may simply leave — a prompt hearing after the decision can satisfy the rule, provided it is a real hearing and not a formality.

What *Maneka Gandhi* did not do is make judges the authors of policy. The court asks whether the procedure is fair and whether the restriction is reasonable; it does not ask whether a wiser law could have been written. The difference between those two questions is the difference between review and rule.

**1.** A statute allows the passport authority to impound any passport "in the interests of the general public". It says expressly that the holder shall never
be told the reasons or heard at any stage. Applying the passage, the statute is:
- (A) valid, because it is a law enacted by the legislature, and Article 21 requires only a procedure established by law
- (B) valid, because the interests of the general public override personal liberty
- (C) invalid, because a procedure that denies any hearing at any stage is not right, just and fair
- (D) invalid, because a passport can never be impounded

- (D) objectionable, because it gave judges too much power over policy
