## Lesson — Critical reasoning: the "multiply it by seven" flaw (≈ 6 min)

**The concept.** The commonest argument in a CLAT logical-reasoning passage is: *"X worked here, so X will work there."* The evidence is real; the leap
is the problem. Examiners call it over-generalisation or a faulty analogy, and nearly every question on such a passage — assumption, weaken, strengthen,
flaw — is about the **gap between the setting where the evidence came from and the setting the conclusion is about**.

**Method — the three-box check.** Before reading the options, draw three boxes in the margin:
1. **Evidence box** — what was measured, *where, when, on whom*.
2. **Conclusion box** — what is being claimed, *where, when, about whom*.
3. **Gap** — the one word that differs between the boxes (day, city, age group, scale). Then:
   - **Assumption** = "the gap makes no difference."
   - **Weaken** = "the gap does make a difference" (the new setting differs in a way that matters).
   - **Strengthen** = "the gap makes no difference" (the settings are alike in the way that matters), or the evidence is cleaner than it looks.
   - **Flaw** = "treats results from one setting as if they apply unchanged to another."

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *"A State gave free bicycles to girls in Class IX in two districts, and enrolment in Class X there rose by 30%. The
State should therefore give free laptops to all college students to raise college enrolment."*
Evidence box: bicycles · Class IX girls · two districts. Conclusion box: laptops · all college students · whole State. **Gap:** three of them — the
benefit, the age group and the barrier. Bicycles worked by solving *distance*; does a laptop solve whatever keeps students out of college?
*Q: Which is the argument's assumption?* → "That the reason students stay out of college is one a laptop removes, as a bicycle removed distance."
Decoy: "That bicycles caused the rise" — that is an assumption too, but a smaller one; CLAT wants the one that bridges the *gap*.

**The trap.** A "weaken" option that attacks the **evidence** ("the enrolment figures were collected by the department that ran the scheme") sounds strong
but is usually second-best when another option attacks the **gap**. And watch absolute words — "the closure **will certainly** fail" is never the answer.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 7: Article 19 (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 19(1)** → **six freedoms, for citizens only**: (a) speech & expression, (b) assemble peaceably and without arms, (c) form associations/unions
   (co-operative societies added by the **97th Am., 2011**), (d) move freely, (e) reside and settle, (g) any profession, trade or business →
   **(f) property was deleted by the 44th Am. (1978)**.
2. **Art 19(2)** → speech can be limited only by **"reasonable" restrictions** on **eight grounds**: sovereignty & integrity of India (**16th Am. 1963**),
   security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, **public order** (**1st Am. 1951**), decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation,
   incitement to an offence. Clauses **19(3)–(6)** carry the restrictions on (b)–(g).
3. **Press freedom** → not named, but **inside 19(1)(a)** → ***Romesh Thappar* (1950)**: pre-censorship of a journal struck down.
4. ***Shreya Singhal* (2015)** → **s. 66A, IT Act 2000 struck down**: "grossly offensive", "annoyance" were vague and fit none of the 19(2) grounds;
   the Court separated **discussion, advocacy and incitement** — only incitement may be punished.
5. ***Anuradha Bhasin* (2020, 3 judges)** → speech and trade **through the internet** are protected by 19(1)(a)/(g); shutdown orders must be **published,
   proportionate and reviewed**. Also: **Art 358** — Art 19 is suspended **automatically** during a national emergency on war or external aggression.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the list: **19(1)(a)–(g) · 19(2) · Romesh Thappar · Shreya Singhal · Anuradha Bhasin / Art 358.** Say the grounds count (8),
the deleted clause (f) and its amendment (44th), and what each case struck down.

**Case.** ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India*** (2015, J. Chelameswar and R. F. Nariman JJ): struck down s. 66A of the IT Act as vague and outside
Art 19(2); read down s. 79 so that intermediaries must remove content only on a court or government order.

**The trap.** Two absolutes: (1) "Art 19 rights are available to **every person**" — no, **citizens only** (contrast Art 14 and Art 21: any person);
(2) "*Anuradha Bhasin* made **internet access a fundamental right**" — it did not; it protected speech and trade **using** the internet and demanded
proportionality.

**Passage 1 (Questions 1–5) — 385 words**

When the municipal corporation of a mid-sized western city closed four streets of its old market to private cars on Sundays last spring, the shopkeepers' association predicted ruin. Six months later the corporation's own figures tell a different story. Footfall counted by cameras at the market's three gates rose by 22 per cent on closed Sundays compared with the same Sundays a year earlier. Sales-tax receipts from the 140 registered shops in the zone rose by 9 per cent over the same period, against 4 per cent for the city as a whole. Air-quality sensors on the main street recorded fine-particle levels a third lower on closed days than on open ones.

The corporation now proposes to extend the closure to every day of the week, and the case for doing so seems, at first glance, overwhelming. If a car-free Sunday brings more shoppers, more trade and cleaner air, a car-free week should bring seven times the benefit. The objection of the shopkeepers, who still insist that their regular customers arrive by car, has, the corporation says, been answered by the numbers.

Yet the numbers answer a narrower question than the one now being asked. Sunday shoppers in the old market are largely families out for the day; many come by bus or on foot from the surrounding neighbourhoods, and they come as much for the street food and the atmosphere as for any particular purchase. Weekday customers are different. Wholesale buyers from nearby towns come to the cloth and hardware merchants with vans to load; office workers stop on their way home. For them, a closed street is not a pleasant promenade but an obstacle.

There is also the question of where the cars went. The corporation measured air quality inside the closed zone but not on the ring road around it, where Sunday traffic was diverted. A daily closure would push weekday traffic, far heavier than Sunday traffic, onto the same ring road.

None of this means the closure should be abandoned. It means that a policy shown to work on one day, for one kind of visitor, cannot simply be multiplied by seven. The sensible course is a weekday trial on two days, with loading permits for wholesale vans and sensors on the ring road, before the corporation commits the whole week.

- (A) The Sunday closure of the old market should be abandoned because it harmed weekday trade.
- (B) The success of the Sunday closure does not by itself justify a daily closure, which should first be tried on a limited scale.
- (C) The Sunday closure raised footfall, sales and air quality in the old market.
- (D) The shopkeepers were wrong to claim that their regular customers arrive by car.

**2.** The corporation's proposal, as described in the second paragraph, depends on which of the following assumptions?

- (C) Air quality in the old market is worse on weekdays than on Sundays.
- (D) Sales-tax receipts are the best measure of a market's prosperity.

When Prime Minister Narendra Modi landed in Manila on 16 November 2026, he walked into the busiest diplomatic week of the Philippine year. As ASEAN Chair for 2026, the Philippines was hosting both the 23rd ASEAN-India Summit and the 21st East Asia Summit on 17 November. India and the Southeast Asian bloc have designated 2026 the ASEAN-India Year of [1], and in 2027 they will mark 35 years of dialogue relations and five years of their Comprehensive Strategic Partnership.

The relationship began modestly. India became a sectoral dialogue partner of ASEAN in 1992, a year after New Delhi announced its Look East Policy in the shadow of a balance-of-payments crisis. Summit-level meetings followed in 2002. In [2], the policy was renamed Act East, a change meant to signal that India would do more than watch the region: it would build roads, ports and security ties there. In his departure statement, Mr Modi called ASEAN the heart of that policy and central to India's MAHASAGAR vision for the Indian Ocean.

The East Asia Summit is a different creature. Established in 2005, it brings the ASEAN members together with eight partners: India, China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, the United States and Russia. It is a forum for leaders rather than an organisation with a secretariat of its own, and it works by consensus. That makes it useful for conversation but slow at producing binding commitments; disputes in the South China Sea have been discussed there for two decades without a settled code of conduct.

Trade remains the sore point. India's imports from ASEAN have grown faster than its exports, and New Delhi has long pressed for a review of the goods trade agreement that came into force in 2010. Indian negotiators argue that its rules of origin are too loose, letting goods made outside the bloc enter India at ASEAN tariff rates. Southeast Asian governments reply that India's own tariffs and standards rules make its market harder to enter than it looks.

Maritime cooperation is where interests overlap most clearly. Several ASEAN members contest Chinese claims at sea, and India has offered coastal surveillance, joint naval exercises and, in the Philippines' case, BrahMos missiles. Whether that cooperation can deepen without either side being drawn into the other's disputes is the question Manila was expected to leave open.
