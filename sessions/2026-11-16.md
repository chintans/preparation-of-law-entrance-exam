## Lesson — Institution stories: pre-load four slots (≈ 6 min)

**Why it matters.** At least one CLAT current-affairs passage a year is about an *institution* — a court, a commission, a regulator — rather than an
event. Those passages are dense with nouns that hide static questions: the Article behind the body, who heads it, how many members it has,
which report or judgment shaped it.

**Method — four slots, filled before you read the questions (≈ 30 s).** For every institution the passage names, write in the margin:
1. **Source** — the Article (constitutional body) or the Act and section (statutory body). This one fact decides half the questions.
2. **Head now** — the current chair/chief (and who appoints him or her).
3. **Number** — members, judges, the term, the majority needed.
4. **Shaping text** — the report or case examiners pair with it.
Then read the questions and match each stem to a slot. A stem that fits no slot is usually answered *in* the passage — go back for it.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** A passage line: *"Opposition parties again questioned the Commission's neutrality after the Chief Election
Commissioner extended the voter-roll revision deadline."* Slots: **Source** Art 324 · **Head** CEC **Gyanesh Kumar** (since Feb 2025) · **Number** CEC +
2 ECs; chosen by a committee of the PM, the Leader of the Opposition and a Union Minister (2023 Act) · **Shaping text** *Anoop Baranwal* (2023).
Question: *"The CEC can be removed from office — (a) by the President on the PM's advice (b) in like manner and on like grounds as a Supreme Court
judge (c) by a resolution of the Lok Sabha alone (d) by the selection committee that appointed him."* The **Source** slot answers it: **Art 324(5)
→ (b)**. The other ECs are removable on the CEC's recommendation — the classic look-alike.

**The traps.** (1) **Two Articles, one body** — the Supreme Court is *created* by Art 124 (judges, number fixed by Parliament by law), *sits* under
**Art 130**, *makes rules* under Art 145, and *binds* other courts under Art 141. The stem tells you which. (2) **Constitutional vs statutory** — an
inquiry commission (CoI Act 1952) or the Data Protection Board (DPDP Act 2023) has no Article; an option offering one is a decoy. (3) **Report
numbers** — the Law Commission writes many reports in the same year; the *topic* picks the number, not the year.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 6: Articles 14–18, equality (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 14** → equality before law + equal protection of the laws, for **any person**. Classification is allowed only if it has an **intelligible
   differentia** with a **rational nexus** to the law's object (*Anwar Ali Sarkar*, 1952).
2. **Arbitrariness is the opposite of equality** → ***E. P. Royappa* (1974)**; carried into Art 21 by ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978)**.
3. **Art 15** (citizens; no discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth) → **15(4)** backward classes/SC/ST (**1st Am. 1951**, after
   *Champakam Dorairajan*) · **15(5)** admissions incl. private unaided (**93rd Am. 2005**) · **15(6) + 16(6)** EWS, up to **10%** (**103rd Am. 2019**).
4. **Art 16** (public employment) → **16(4)** backward-class reservation in posts — ***Indra Sawhney* (1992, 9 judges)**: 50% ceiling, creamy layer,
   no reservation in promotion → reversed for SC/ST by **16(4A)** (**77th Am. 1995**).
5. **Art 17** abolishes untouchability (enforced through the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955**) · **Art 18** abolishes titles — ***Balaji
   Raghavan* (1996)**: Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are **not** titles, but may not be used as a prefix or suffix.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts: **14 · 15(4) / 15(5) / 15(6) · 16(4) / 16(4A) / 16(6) · 17 · 18.** Say the amendment for each sub-clause.

**Case.** *Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (2022, **5 judges, 3:2**): upheld the **103rd Amendment** (EWS quota) as consistent with the basic
structure — including the exclusion of SC/ST/OBC from the EWS category.

**The trap.** EWS sits in **15(6) and 16(6)** — not 15(5) (private-college admissions) and not 16(4A) (promotions). And **Art 14 is for "any person"**;
Arts 15 and 16 are for citizens only.

**Passage 1 (Questions 1–5) — 427 words**

Should India's Supreme Court be split in two? An opinion piece published on 14 November 2026 revived a question that has circulated for nearly four decades. Its argument begins with numbers. The Court now has a sanctioned strength of 38 judges, raised from 34 earlier this year, yet more than 90,000 cases are pending before it. Most are ordinary appeals — property disputes, bail matters, service claims — that have already been through at least two courts.

The framers had a narrower picture in mind. They expected the Court to settle the great questions of constitutional meaning through benches of five or more judges, as Article 145(3) requires. In the 1960s such benches delivered well over a hundred judgments a year; today the figure is often in single digits. The author's diagnosis is that a court trying to correct every error ends up with too little time to say what the law is.

The remedy proposed is structural. A Constitutional Court of eleven judges would sit in Delhi and hear only constitutional questions and matters of national importance. Four or five regional Courts of Appeal would hear the ordinary appeals, bringing the highest tier of justice closer to litigants from the south, the east and the north-east, who must now travel to Delhi and pay Delhi lawyers.

The idea is not new. In 2009 the Law Commission, in its [1] Report, recommended a Constitution Bench at Delhi and cassation benches in four regions. A parliamentary standing committee endorsed regional benches in 2023. In 2016 a three-judge bench, hearing V. Vasanthakumar v. H. C. Bhatia, referred the question of a National Court of Appeal to a Constitution Bench. That reference has never been heard.

Part of the plan may not need an amendment at all. Article [2] already allows the Court to sit in Delhi "or in such other place or places" as the Chief Justice of India may appoint with the approval of the President. Regional benches of the same Court could be created this way. A separate Court of Appeal whose decisions were final, however, would be a new court, and creating one would require Parliament to amend the Constitution.

The Court itself has been cool to the idea. Its judges worry that benches scattered across the country would drift apart, producing conflicting rulings in a system where the law declared by the Supreme Court binds every court in India. Supporters reply that a court which takes years to decide a bail appeal has already given up on uniformity of a more basic kind: equal access to justice.
