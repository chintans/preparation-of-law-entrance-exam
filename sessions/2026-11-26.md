## Lesson — Assumptions: the negation test (≈ 6 min)

**What an assumption is.** An assumption is a premise the argument **needs** but never states. Take it away and the conclusion falls. It is not something that merely
**helps** the argument; that would be a strengthener. CLAT asks for it in several ways: *"the argument assumes…"*, *"…depends on which of the following?"*, *"which is a
presupposition?"*

**The method, in three steps.**
1. **Find the gap.** Write the argument as *evidence → conclusion*. Look for the new idea in the conclusion that the evidence never mentions. The assumption bridges it.
2. **Shortlist** the options that talk about that gap. Drop options about side issues.
3. **Negation test.** Turn each shortlisted option into its opposite. If the argument now collapses, that option is the assumption. If the argument survives, it isn't.

**Worked example.** *"The new metro line will reduce traffic jams on MG Road, because it runs parallel to MG Road."*
- The gap: running parallel → fewer cars. The missing link is that **drivers will switch** to the metro.
- (A) "The metro will be cheaper than a taxi." Negate it: the metro is not cheaper. Drivers might still switch for speed, so the argument survives. **Not required.**
- (B) "At least some people who now drive on MG Road will use the metro instead." Negate it: **no** driver switches. Then traffic cannot fall, and the argument collapses.
  **This is the assumption.**
- (C) "All MG Road drivers will switch." Negate it: not all of them switch. Some still might, so the argument survives. That makes it too strong to be **needed**.

**The traps examiners set**
- **The strengthener in disguise.** "Metro fares will be subsidised" helps the argument but isn't needed. Negation exposes it.
- **The extreme word.** *All, always, only, never* are usually too strong for an assumption. *Some, at least, not entirely* usually survive the negation test.
- **The restated premise.** An option that repeats the evidence is not an assumption, because the argument has already stated it.
- **Whose argument?** In a passage, check whether the question asks about the author's argument or someone else's (the "supporters", the "critics"). Q2 and Q4 today
  ask about different people.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 11: Writs (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 32** → the SC issues writs **only to enforce Fundamental Rights**. The right to move the SC is itself a Fundamental Right; Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the
   Constitution. It cannot be suspended except as the Constitution provides (Art 32(4), read with Art 359).
2. **Art 226** → a High Court can issue writs for Fundamental Rights **"and for any other purpose"**. That makes it **wider** than Art 32, though it is discretionary.
3. **Habeas corpus** ("produce the body") → frees a person from unlawful detention. It lies against the State **and against private persons**.
4. **Mandamus** ("we command") → orders a public authority to perform a **public duty**. It does **not** lie against the **President or a Governor** (Art 361), against
   private persons, or to enforce a private contract.
5. **Prohibition vs certiorari vs quo warranto:**
   - **prohibition** stops a lower court or tribunal **before** it decides (*while the case is pending*);
   - **certiorari** **quashes** an order **after** it is made;
   - **quo warranto** asks "by what authority" someone holds a **substantive public office**. **Any person** may apply, even if not personally aggrieved.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the lines and say what each does: **32 · 226 · habeas corpus · mandamus · prohibition/certiorari**.

**Case:** ***ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976, 5 judges, 4:1).** During the Emergency the majority held that no one could move a court for habeas corpus while
Art 21 was suspended. Khanna J was the lone dissent. A nine-judge bench overruled it in ***Puttaswamy* (2017)**, and since the **44th Amendment (1978)**, Arts 20 and 21
cannot be suspended even in an Emergency (Art 359).

**The trap:** "Prohibition can be issued after the order is passed" is wrong; that is certiorari. "Mandamus lies against the President" is wrong (Art 361).
"Quo warranto needs an aggrieved petitioner" is wrong: any person can apply. "Art 32 is wider than Art 226" reverses the rule.

A State government has proposed that every district court hold an evening shift, from 5 PM to 8 PM, three days a week. The shift would hear only petty cases: traffic challans, cheque-bounce complaints and small civil claims. Its supporters say the plan will cut the backlog of such cases by half within two years, and they point to a pilot run in two districts last year, where pending petty cases fell by forty per cent in twelve months.

The argument is attractive, but it rests on more than the numbers show. The evening courts will be staffed by retired judges, who will be paid a fixed fee for each sitting. The government's case, therefore, is not simply that more hours will produce more disposals. It is that the retired judges are willing to sit in large enough numbers, and that lawyers and litigants will actually turn up after office hours. In the pilot districts both conditions held. Both districts are compact, with the court complex within a short bus ride of most villages. A litigant in a large hill district who must travel three hours each way may find an evening hearing harder to attend, not easier.

There is a second difficulty. The pilot ran alongside a special drive by the police to serve summonses on time. Half of all adjournments in petty cases happen because the accused has not been served, and the drive fixed that problem in both districts. If the summons drive, and not the extra hours, produced most of the fall, then extending evening courts across the State without a similar drive may achieve little.

None of this means the plan is wrong. Evening courts have a real advantage: a daily-wage worker who would lose a day's pay to attend a morning hearing may be able to come after work. The plan's critics, who argue that the money would be better spent appointing new regular judges, have not explained how new posts could be filled quickly when hundreds of sanctioned posts already lie vacant. The sensible course is to extend the scheme in stages, district by district, and to measure the result separately from any summons drive, so that the State learns what is actually working before it spends money everywhere.
