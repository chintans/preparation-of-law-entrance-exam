# Day 4 — Critical reasoning: the gap between premise and conclusion (AILET Logical Reasoning)

## Lesson

**The concept.** Every critical-reasoning (CR) item is a short argument: **premises** (the evidence) →
**conclusion** (the claim). The conclusion almost always says *more* than the premises prove. That
missing piece is the **gap**, and almost every question type is about it:

| Question asks for | What you do with the gap |
|---|---|
| Assumption | name the unstated bridge the author needs |
| Strengthen | make the bridge sturdier |
| Weaken | knock the bridge out (show another way the evidence could arise) |
| Flaw | describe why the bridge does not hold |
| Conclusion / inference | find what the premises actually support — no gap allowed |

**Method (four steps, ~60 s).**
1. Read the question stem first — know what you are hunting for.
2. Find the conclusion (look for *therefore, so, hence, thus, should*). Then ask: **"Why might this be
   false even if every premise is true?"** That is the gap.
3. Predict an answer in your own words before reading the options.
4. **Negation test for assumptions:** flip the option into its opposite. If the argument collapses, it is a
   *necessary* assumption. If the argument survives, it isn't.

**Three gap patterns that cover most AILET/CLAT items.**
- **Cause–effect.** "A happened, then B; so A caused B." Weakeners: another cause, reverse causation, coincidence.
  Strengtheners: a comparison group where A was absent and B did not happen.
- **Generalisation / analogy.** "It worked here, so it will work there." Assumption: the two cases are alike in
  the way that matters.
- **Conditional logic.** "All A are B" does **not** give "All B are A". If every topper studies daily, a
  student who studies daily is not thereby a topper.

**Worked example (AILET style).**
*A State banned single-use plastic bags in its capital and, within a year, the volume of plastic waste
collected from the city's drains fell by 40%. The State therefore plans to extend the ban statewide,
expecting a similar fall in drain waste everywhere.*
Which is an assumption on which the plan depends?
(A) Plastic bags are the largest single source of all waste in the State.
(B) Plastic bags make up a similar share of drain waste in other towns as they did in the capital.
(C) The ban in the capital was obeyed by every shopkeeper.
(D) Paper bags are cheaper than plastic bags.

*Working:* gap type = generalisation (it worked here → it will work there). Predict: "other places are
alike in the relevant way." Negate (B): "bags make up a far smaller share of drain waste elsewhere" — then
the ban cannot be expected to produce a similar fall. Argument collapses → **(B)**. (A) is not needed (bags
need not be the largest source of *all* waste); (C) is too extreme — a 40% fall happened without proof of
total compliance; (D) is out of scope.

**The traps examiners set.**
- **Extreme words** ("all", "never", "every", "only") in assumption options — usually more than the author needs.
- **"Some"** options in weaken/strengthen questions — true but too weak to move the argument.
- **Restating a premise** as the conclusion. The main conclusion is what the *other* sentences support.
- **Out of scope:** cost, popularity, feasibility — when the argument is about *whether something works*.
- **Reversed conditionals** (see the third pattern above).

## Test — 8 questions, aim for ~8 minutes, +1 / −0.25

**1.** After the city installed speed cameras on its Ring Road, accidents on that road fell by 30%. The
city should therefore install speed cameras on all its major roads to cut accidents across the city.
The argument assumes that:
- (A) Speed cameras cost less to run than posting traffic police.
- (B) Speeding contributes to accidents on other major roads much as it did on the Ring Road.
- (C) No accidents occur on roads fitted with speed cameras.
- (D) The Ring Road had the highest number of accidents in the city.

**2.** A college survey found that students who use the library every day score higher in examinations than
those who do not. The college concludes that library use improves exam performance and plans to make daily
library visits compulsory. Which of the following, if true, most **weakens** the college's conclusion?
- (A) The library added 2,000 new books last year.
- (B) Daily library users are mostly students who already put in more hours of study overall, wherever they study.
- (C) Some students who never visit the library also score well.
- (D) Compulsory visits would require the library to stay open longer.

**3.** Online grocery orders in a town rose sharply in the six months after a new delivery app was launched
there. The app's makers claim that the app caused the rise. Which of the following, if true, most
**strengthens** the claim?
- (A) The app has an average user rating of 4.5 stars.
- (B) Several new supermarkets opened in the town during the same six months.
- (C) In a neighbouring town of similar size and income, where the app was not available, online grocery
  orders stayed flat over the same six months.
- (D) Most adults in the town own a smartphone.

**4.** *Many argue that a shorter working week will reduce output. But a firm's output depends less on the
hours its people work than on how productive each hour is. Trials in several firms found that tired
employees make more errors, which then have to be corrected. Hence a moderately shorter working week need
not reduce a firm's output.*
Which of the following is the **main conclusion** of the argument?
- (A) A shorter working week will reduce output.
- (B) A firm's output depends more on productivity per hour than on hours worked.
- (C) Tired employees make more errors that have to be corrected.
- (D) A moderately shorter working week need not reduce a firm's output.

**5.** The new metro line will reduce traffic congestion on MG Road, because thousands of commuters who now
drive along MG Road will switch to the metro. The argument depends on which assumption?
- (A) Travelling by metro will be cheaper than driving.
- (B) The road space freed by those commuters will not soon be taken up by an equal volume of new traffic.
- (C) Every commuter who now drives on MG Road will switch to the metro.
- (D) The metro line will be completed on schedule.

**6.** Every member of the national cricket team follows a strict diet. Arjun follows a strict diet.
Therefore Arjun is a member of the national cricket team.
The reasoning is flawed because it:
- (A) treats a feature shared by every member of a group as if only members of that group could have it.
- (B) draws a general conclusion from too small a sample.
- (C) attacks Arjun's character instead of addressing his argument.
- (D) mistakes an effect for its cause.

**7.** All candidates who scored above 90 were shortlisted. No shortlisted candidate failed the interview.
Meera failed the interview. Which of the following **must be true**?
- (A) Meera scored above 90.
- (B) Meera was shortlisted.
- (C) Meera did not score above 90.
- (D) Meera scored below 50.

**8.** *Principle:* Under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, an acceptance may be revoked at any time before the
communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor (that is, before it comes to the
knowledge of the proposer), but not afterwards. A revocation is complete as against the person to whom it is
made when it comes to his knowledge.
*Facts:* Rohan offers by letter to sell his car to Priya. On 5 June Priya posts a letter of acceptance. On
6 June she changes her mind and emails Rohan revoking her acceptance; Rohan reads the email the same day.
Her acceptance letter reaches Rohan on 8 June. Which statement is correct?
- (A) A contract was formed on 5 June: once Priya posted her acceptance, it was complete against Rohan.
- (B) No contract: Priya's revocation reached Rohan before her acceptance did, so it was validly revoked.
- (C) A contract was formed: an acceptance, once posted, cannot be revoked.
- (D) No contract: Rohan never confirmed that he had received Priya's acceptance.

## Answer key (do not send)

| Q | Ans | Tag | Why | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | LR-CR | Generalisation gap: other roads must be alike in the relevant cause (speeding). Negate → plan fails. | (C) extreme; (D) not needed; (A) out of scope. |
| 2 | B | LR-CR | Alternative cause: total study hours explain both library use and scores. | (C) "some" — too weak; (D) feasibility, not effectiveness. |
| 3 | C | LR-CR | Comparison group without the app shows no rise → isolates the app as cause. | (B) weakens (alternative cause); (D) background condition, barely moves it. |
| 4 | D | LR-CR | "Hence…" — supported by the other sentences. | (B) intermediate premise; (A) the view being rebutted. |
| 5 | B | LR-CR | Negate: freed space quickly refilled → no reduction → argument collapses. | (C) extreme (some switching suffices); (D) timing not in the conclusion; (A) premise already says they will switch. |
| 6 | A | LR-CR | Reversed conditional (affirming the consequent): all members → diet ≠ diet → member. | (B) no sample involved; (D) cause/effect not the issue. |
| 7 | C | LR-CR | Contrapositive chain: failed interview → not shortlisted → not above 90. | (A)/(B) reverse the chain; (D) not established. |
| 8 | B | LEG-CONT (review) | Acceptance can be revoked until it reaches the proposer (8 Jun); revocation reached him 6 Jun → valid → no contract. | (A)/(C) confuse the proposer's clock (posting binds the proposer) with the acceptor's (receipt). |
