## Lesson — Rule → exception → proviso (≈ 8 min)

**The idea.** Most CLAT legal passages are built in layers: a **rule** (what the offence is), an **exception** (when it is something less),
and **provisos** (when the exception itself is switched off). Students lose marks by stopping at the first layer that seems to fit. Murder
is the classic example — and CLAT has used it repeatedly.

**The law, in three layers (BNS numbering; IPC in brackets).**
- **Rule.** Culpable homicide — causing death with the intention or knowledge set out in **s. 100 (IPC 299)**. It is **murder** in the
  graver cases listed in **s. 101 (IPC 300)**, punished under **s. 103 (IPC 302)**.
- **Exception 1 to s. 101.** Not murder if the offender, deprived of self-control by **grave AND sudden** provocation, kills the person who
  provoked him (or another person by mistake or accident). He is still guilty of **culpable homicide not amounting to murder, s. 105
  (IPC 304)** — never acquitted.
- **Provisos — the exception does NOT apply** if the provocation was (i) **sought** by the offender as an excuse; (ii) given by something done
  **in obedience to law** or by a **public servant lawfully** using his powers; (iii) given by a **lawful act of private defence**.

**Method — four questions, in order.**
1. Is there a death caused with the required intention/knowledge? (Rule met → start from murder.)
2. Was the provocation **grave** (would a reasonable person of the accused's class lose control)?
3. Was it **sudden**, and did the killing follow **before passion cooled**? Any gap used to plan — fetch a weapon, travel, wait — kills the exception.
4. Does a **proviso** switch the exception off? Only then pick the answer.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *R's landlord tells him at 10 AM that R's wife has been seeing his friend S. R goes home, takes his father's
licensed gun from a locked cupboard, drives to S's office and shoots him at 1 PM.* Step 2: grave — yes. Step 3: sudden? R had three hours,
fetched a weapon and travelled — passion had time to cool → **murder**. This is the shape of *K. M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra* (SC,
1961): the Court applied the "reasonable person of the same class" test and held that the time Nanavati took to fetch a revolver and find the
victim showed deliberation, so the exception failed.

**The trap.** (1) Options that say "provoked, therefore **not guilty**" — the exception lowers the offence; it does not acquit. (2) **Grave but
not sudden** (or sudden but trivial) — both words must hold. (3) The **proviso** buried at the end: lawful arrest, lawful private defence, or a
quarrel the accused picked on purpose.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 4: Preamble, second pass (≈ 4 min)

*Repeated because yesterday's session was missed — this is the one unit examiners ask about almost every year.*

**Five flash facts**
1. **Five descriptors, in order** → **Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic**. Ideals: justice (social, economic, political), liberty
   (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), equality (status, opportunity), fraternity (dignity of the individual; **unity and integrity**).
2. **42nd Amendment, 1976** → added **Socialist, Secular** and **"and integrity"** — the only amendment to the Preamble. *Dr Balram Singh v. UoI*
   (25 Nov 2024): challenge to these additions dismissed.
3. **Objectives Resolution** → moved by **Nehru, 13 Dec 1946**; adopted **22 Jan 1947**.
4. **26 Nov 1949** → adopted, enacted and given to ourselves (Constitution Day); **26 Jan 1950** → in force.
5. **Part of the Constitution?** → *Berubari* (1960): **no** → *Kesavananda* (1973): **yes**, amendable under **Art 368** but not so as to damage the
   basic structure (*LIC v. Consumer Education*, 1995, agreed). Not enforceable in court by itself.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts: **S-S-S-D-R · 42nd 1976 · 13 Dec 1946 · 26 Nov 1949 · Berubari → Kesavananda.** Say each aloud, then check.

**Case.** *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973, **13 judges, 7:6**): the Preamble is part of the Constitution and helps identify the
basic structure, which no amendment may destroy.

**The trap.** "Unity" was there in 1950 — only **"integrity"** was added. And *Berubari*'s "not part" is no longer the law.

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 405 words**

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, a person commits culpable homicide if he causes death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that he is likely by that act to cause death. Culpable homicide becomes murder in the graver cases the Sanhita lists: broadly, where the act is done with the intention of causing death, or of causing an injury the offender knows is likely to kill that particular person, or one sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, or where the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death.

The Sanhita then carves out exceptions. Under the first, culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, while deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation, or of any other person by mistake or accident. The exception is itself fenced in by three provisos. It does not apply where the provocation was sought or voluntarily provoked by the offender as an excuse for killing or doing harm. It does not apply where the provocation was given by anything done in obedience to the law, or by a public servant in the lawful exercise of his powers. Nor does it apply where the provocation was given by anything done in the lawful exercise of the right of private defence. Whether the provocation was grave and sudden enough is a question of fact.

Courts read the exception narrowly. The test is whether a reasonable person of the same class of society as the accused, placed in the same situation, would have been so provoked as to lose self-control. Gravity alone is not enough: the killing must follow before passion has had time to cool. An interval in which the accused could plan, such as fetching a weapon from elsewhere, making enquiries or travelling to find the victim, points to deliberation rather than loss of control. Words and gestures can amount to grave provocation, and earlier conduct of the deceased may explain the accused's state of mind, but the final act of provocation must still be sudden.

An offender who falls within the exception is not acquitted. He is guilty of culpable homicide not amounting to murder, a lesser offence carrying a lighter sentence than murder.
