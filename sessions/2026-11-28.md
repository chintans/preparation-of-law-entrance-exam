## Constitution Capsule: Unit 12, DPSPs and Fundamental Duties (≈ 4 min, before the clock)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 37** → DPSPs (Part IV, **Arts 36–51**) are **not enforceable by any court**, but they are **"fundamental in the governance of the country"** and the State must
   apply them in making laws.
2. **Which amendment added what.**
   - **42nd Am. (1976)** added **39A** (equal justice, free legal aid), **43A** (workers in management) and **48A** (environment, forests, wildlife).
   - **44th Am. (1978)** added **38(2)** (reduce inequalities in income).
   - **97th Am. (2011)** added **43B** (co-operative societies).
3. **Key Articles to match.**
   - **Art 39(b)/(c)**: material resources of the community, and no concentration of wealth. In *Property Owners Assn.* (2024, 9 judges), not every private resource counts.
   - **40**: village panchayats.
   - **44**: Uniform Civil Code.
   - **45**: early childhood care to age six (recast by the 86th Am.).
   - **50**: separate the judiciary from the executive.
   - **51**: international peace.
4. **Art 51A (Part IVA)** → **10 Fundamental Duties** came in with the **42nd Am. 1976**, on the **Swaran Singh Committee's** recommendation. The **11th, 51A(k)**
   (parents or guardians to give a child of 6–14 opportunities for education), came with the **86th Am. 2002**. The duties bind **citizens** only.
5. **The Gandhian, socialist and liberal clusters** (examiners ask you to classify).
   - **Gandhian**: 40 panchayats, 43 cottage industries, 46 weaker sections, 47 prohibition, 48 cow protection.
   - **Socialist**: 38, 39, 41, 42, 43.
   - **Liberal-intellectual**: 44 UCC, 45, 48A, 50, 51.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the lines and say what each does: **37 · 39A/43A/48A · 44 · 50 · 51A(k)**.

**Case:** ***Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980, 5 judges).** The Court struck down the 42nd Am. clauses that gave **all** DPSPs primacy over Arts 14 and 19 (the
extended Art 31C) and that barred review of amendments (Art 368(4)–(5)). The **harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs is basic structure**.

**The trap:**
- "Courts can enforce Art 44" is wrong: DPSPs are non-justiciable.
- "Fundamental Duties were in the original Constitution" is wrong: they came in 1976.
- "There are 10 duties" is out of date: there are 11.
- "39A was added by the 44th Am." is wrong: it was the 42nd.
- "Duties apply to everyone" is wrong: they bind citizens only.

For most of the last century, the student who took notes by hand was simply doing what students had always done. The arrival of the laptop in the lecture hall turned this habit into a choice, and choices invite argument. Those who favour the keyboard point to speed. A practised typist can record nearly everything a lecturer says, while the person with a pen must leave much of it behind. More words, the reasoning goes, mean more material to revise from, and more material means better results.

The argument is attractive, but it rests on a confusion between recording and understanding. Because the hand is slow, the writer cannot copy; she must decide, sentence by sentence, what matters, and put it in her own words. That act of selection is not a loss forced on her by a clumsy tool. It is the learning itself. The typist, freed from the need to choose, often becomes a transcriber, and a transcript, however complete, is something the mind has passed through rather than worked on.

None of this means that the pen is magic. A student who copies slides word for word on paper gains little, and a student who types brief summaries in his own phrasing may do as well as anyone. What matters is not the instrument but the amount of thinking that the instrument permits or demands. The pen happens to demand more; the keyboard merely permits it.

There is a second cost that the keyboard's defenders rarely mention. A laptop is not only a note-taking device. It is also a window to messages, news and every other distraction, and the student who glances away for a moment pays for it, as do the students sitting behind her, whose eyes follow the bright screen. A classroom is a shared space, and a habit that seems private can carry costs for others.

The sensible conclusion is neither a ban nor indifference. Teachers who forbid laptops outright punish the students for whom typing is a genuine need, and those who ignore the question leave the rest to drift. The better course is to teach students what good notes do: they compress, they connect, and they translate the lecturer's language into the learner's. A student who understands that will choose her tool wisely, and use either one well.

### Legal reasoning

**Principle A (Questions 9–11), 54 words.** Whoever intentionally puts any person in fear of any injury to that person or to any other, and thereby dishonestly
induces the person so put in fear to deliver to any person any property, commits extortion. Delivery must be caused by the fear; property obtained without such
fear-induced delivery is not obtained by extortion.

**9.** *Facts:* A tells B, a shopkeeper, that he will spread a false and disgraceful story about B's daughter unless B pays him ₹50,000. Frightened for his
daughter's reputation, B pays. Applying the principle:
- (A) A has not committed extortion, because the threatened injury was to B's daughter, not to B.
- (B) A has not committed extortion, because injury to reputation is not injury.
- (C) A has committed extortion only if the story is published.
- (D) A has committed extortion, because B delivered the money out of fear of injury to his daughter that A intentionally caused.

**10.** *Facts:* A, waving a toy pistol, demands ₹500 from B. B sees at once that the pistol is a toy and is not afraid, but hands over ₹500 because he knows A is deep
in debt and feels sorry for him. Applying the principle:
- (A) A has committed extortion, because he intended to frighten B.
- (B) A has not committed extortion, because B's delivery was not induced by fear.
- (C) A has committed extortion, because B delivered property after a threat.
- (D) A has not committed extortion, because ₹500 is a trivial sum.

**11.** *Facts:* While B is asleep on a train, A slides B's phone out from under his pillow and walks away. Applying **only** the principle:
- (A) A has not committed extortion, because B was not put in fear and did not deliver the phone.
- (B) A has committed extortion, because B would have been afraid had he woken.
- (C) A has committed extortion, because he obtained B's property dishonestly.
- (D) A has not committed extortion, because a phone is not property.

**Principle B (Questions 12–13), 57 words.** No person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the
act charged as an offence, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence.

**12.** *Facts:* X takes a bribe in March 2025, when the maximum punishment is 5 years. In December 2026 an amendment raises the maximum to 10 years. At X's trial in
2027 the court sentences him to 8 years. Applying the principle:
- (A) The sentence is valid, because the law in force at the time of sentencing applies.
- (B) X must be acquitted, because the law changed after the act.
- (C) The 8-year sentence cannot stand; X can be sentenced to at most 5 years.
- (D) The sentence is valid if the amendment expressly says it applies to past offences.

**13.** *Facts:* Y commits an offence in 2025. A 2026 law transfers the trial of all such offences, including pending ones, to a new special court with a simplified
procedure. Punishment is unchanged. Y says the transfer violates the principle. Y's objection:
- (A) succeeds, because any law that operates on past acts is barred.
- (B) succeeds, because Y had a right to be tried by the court that existed in 2025.
- (C) fails, because the principle applies only to civil laws.
- (D) fails, because the principle bars retrospective conviction and heavier penalty, not changes in procedure or forum.
