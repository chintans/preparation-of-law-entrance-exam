## Lesson — Direction sense and blood relations: draw, net, check (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** Analytical reasoning is due and at 50%. Both of your misses here were the last step, not the method: on Day 22 you added
the legs of a walk (14 km) instead of netting them (10 km); on Day 17 you stopped a family chain one generation early ("father" for
"father's son"). AILET puts these inside its 70 reasoning questions at 48 s each — they are free marks if you **draw every time**.

**Method 1 — directions on a grid.**
1. Start at (0, 0), north = up. Write each leg as a move: N +y, S −y, E +x, W −x.
2. **Turns depend on the way you face.** Right of north is east; right of east is south; right of south is west; right of west is north.
   Left is the reverse. Write the new facing *before* the distance.
3. **Net, don't add.** Total x and total y separately; distance = √(x² + y²). Learn the triples: **3-4-5, 6-8-10, 5-12-13, 8-15-17**.
4. **Direction of the end point** from the start: sign of x gives E/W, sign of y gives N/S (x = +8, y = −6 → south-east).
5. **Rotations in degrees:** clockwise = to the right. N 0°, E 90°, S 180°, W 270°; add clockwise, subtract anticlockwise, mod 360.
   45° steps give NE (45), SE (135), SW (225), NW (315).

**Method 2 — blood relations as a tree.**
1. Symbols: square/“+” male, circle/“−” female, “=” married couple, a vertical line one generation down, a horizontal line siblings.
2. **Translate from the far end.** "My father's father's only son" → father's father = grandfather → his only son = **my father**.
3. **Fix gender only when the text gives it.** "A is the child of B" says nothing about A's sex; "brother", "son", "Mr" do.
4. **Count the generations on the drawing** before you name the relation — this is where "one generation short" happens.
5. Coded relations (P + Q = P is brother of Q): **decode each symbol into a tree edge, then read the last link.**

**Worked example (AILET style).** *Kiran walks 5 km west, turns left and walks 9 km, turns left and walks 13 km, turns left and walks 3 km. How far is she from the
start, and in which direction?*
→ W 5: (−5, 0). Left of west = south, 9: (−5, −9). Left of south = east, 13: (8, −9). Left of east = north, 3: (8, −6).
Net x = +8, y = −6 → 6-8-10 triple → **10 km, south-east**. The trap options are always the **sum of the legs** (30 km) and the
**mirror direction** (north-east / south-west). If your net isn't among the options, re-check one turn — don't pick the nearest number.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **Summed legs** as an option — never pick a number bigger than the longest single leg's combination without netting.
2. **"Left" read from the reader's view, not the walker's** — always turn relative to the current facing.
3. **Generation slips** — "my mother's father's only daughter" is my **mother**, not my aunt.
4. **Assumed gender** — "child", "sibling", "spouse" do not tell you male or female.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 3: Emergency numbers (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 352** → National Emergency on **war, external aggression or armed rebellion** ("internal disturbance" replaced by the **44th
   Amendment, 1978**); needs the Cabinet's written decision; each House must approve within **one month** by **special majority**; then
   continues **six months** at a time.
2. **Art 356** → President's Rule when the State's constitutional machinery fails; each House must approve within **two months** (simple
   majority); continues **six months** at a time; **three years** maximum.
3. **Art 356(5)** → beyond **one year** only if a National Emergency is in operation **and** the Election Commission certifies elections
   cannot be held.
4. **Art 360** → Financial Emergency; approval within **two months**; **never proclaimed**; allows cuts in salaries, including of judges.
5. **Arts 358–359** → 358: Art 19 is suspended automatically (only for war/external aggression since the 44th Am.); 359: the President may
   suspend enforcement of other FRs — but **never Arts 20 and 21**.

**The ladder (say it aloud): 1 – 2 – 6 – 3.** One month (352) · two months (356, 360) · six months each extension · three years maximum (356).

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts and say what each does: **352 · 356 · 356(5) · 360 · 359.** Then check.

**Case.** *S. R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994, **9 judges**): a proclamation under Art 356 is open to judicial review; a government's
majority is tested on the **floor of the House**, not in the Governor's chamber; the Assembly is only kept in suspended animation until
Parliament approves; secularism is part of the basic structure.

**The trap.** You have twice answered **six months** for Art 356 approval — six months is each *continuation*. Approval is **two months**.
The other bait is **one month** — that is Art 352.

**Passage (Questions 1–2) — 374 words**

Long before satellites, sailors crossed oceans with a method so plain that its name sounds almost careless: dead reckoning. The navigator began from a known position, usually the last headland seen from the deck. From then on he needed only three things: the direction the ship was steering, its speed through the water and the time that had passed. Direction came from the compass, speed from a knotted line paid out over the stern while a sandglass ran, and time from the ship's bell. Multiply speed by time, lay the result along the course on the chart, and the new position followed.

The method's strength was its discipline. Every turn of the helm and every change of speed had to be written in the log as it happened, because each new estimate was built on the one before. A navigator who trusted his memory instead of his entries soon found that the ship he had imagined and the ship he was standing on had drifted apart.

Its weakness was the same chain of dependence. Currents carried the hull sideways without anyone feeling it, the wind pushed it off its course, and a slightly wrong compass reading repeated itself on every leg. Errors did not cancel out; they accumulated, so that a small mistake made on the first morning could become many miles of error a week later. For this reason careful navigators treated every dead-reckoning position as provisional. Whenever the sky allowed, they fixed the ship's place afresh from the sun or the stars and started the chain again from that firmer point.

It would be easy to call dead reckoning obsolete. Yet aircraft, submarines and even the phone in a pocket still use its logic whenever the satellite signal is lost, estimating where they are from where they were and how they have moved since. The tools have changed; the reasoning has not.

The lesson reaches beyond the sea. Any calculation that proceeds step by step, from a household budget to a long chain of argument, carries the same risk: one early slip, faithfully copied forward, can look like a confident answer. The navigator's habits of recording each step and checking against a fixed point whenever one is available remain the best defence.
