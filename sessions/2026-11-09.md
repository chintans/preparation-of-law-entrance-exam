## Lesson — Mistake of fact vs mistake of law (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** LEG-PRIN is due today, and the last time a mistake-of-fact principle came up (Day 10, Q3–4) you missed both — once by
not classifying the mistake first, once by importing an outside rule. CLAT gives you the principle; the marks come from classifying
the mistake correctly and checking the qualifier words.

**The rule (BNS ss. 14 and 17 = IPC ss. 76 and 79).** Nothing is an offence if done by a person who is **bound** (s. 14) or **justified**
(s. 17) by law, **or** who, by reason of a **mistake of fact and not of law**, **in good faith** believes he is.

**Method — four questions, in this order:**
1. **What was he wrong about?** The *facts* (who, what, which bag, is that a tiger) → mistake of fact. *What the law allows* → mistake
   of law → stop: no defence (*ignorantia juris non excusat*).
2. **Good faith?** BNS s. 2(11) (IPC s. 52): nothing is in good faith if done **without due care and attention**. Look for the fact that
   shows he checked — or didn't.
3. **If his belief were true, would the act be lawful?** If not, the defence fails even on a genuine mistake.
4. **Bound or justified?** Duty (court officer under a warrant) → s. 14. Permission (citizen stopping an apparent attacker) → s. 17.
   CLAT rarely asks the section number — but options often give the *wrong reason*.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *Principle:* as above. *Facts:* A, a forest guard told to shoot a man-eating leopard, sees movement in
tall grass at dusk, does not wait to see, and fires. He kills a villager. (A) Not liable: a mistake of fact. (B) Not liable: he was bound
to shoot the leopard. (C) Liable: he did not act with due care and attention, so his belief was not in good faith. (D) Liable: ignorance
of law is no excuse. → **(C).** (A) and (B) are true in part but skip the good-faith test; (D) misclassifies — he was wrong about a fact.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **The honest-but-careless actor.** "He genuinely believed…" is not enough — the principle says *good faith*, and good faith needs care.
2. **A law dressed as a fact.** "He thought the new rule had not come into force" is a mistake about law.
3. **Absolutes.** Options with *always*, *never*, *any mistake* — check them against the passage line by line.
4. **Right result, wrong reason.** Two options reach "not liable"; only one gives the principle's reason.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 1: Routes to the courts (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 32** → move the **Supreme Court** for writs — **only to enforce Fundamental Rights**; Art 32 is itself a Fundamental Right
   (Ambedkar: "heart and soul" of the Constitution).
2. **Art 226** → move a **High Court** for writs for Fundamental Rights **"and for any other purpose"** — wider than Art 32 (legal and
   statutory rights too); not itself a Fundamental Right.
3. **Art 131** → Supreme Court's **original and exclusive** jurisdiction in disputes **between the Union and States, or States inter se**,
   on a legal right — never a private citizen's suit (today's Bhakra-Beas case is the type).
4. **Art 143** → the **President** may refer a question of law or fact to the SC for its **opinion**; heard by **at least five judges**
   (**Art 145(3)**); the Court **may decline** to answer (*Ismail Faruqui*, 1994); the opinion is advisory.
5. **Art 136** → **Special Leave Petition**: discretionary appeal from **any court or tribunal** in India — except military tribunals
   (Art 136(2)).

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts above and say what each does: **32 · 226 · 131 · 143 · 136.** Then check.

**Case.** *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997, **7 judges**): judicial review by the High Courts (Art 226) and the Supreme Court
(Art 32) is part of the **basic structure**; tribunals can supplement but never exclude it.

**The trap.** Which is *wider*? Art **226** (FRs + "any other purpose"). Which is a *Fundamental Right*? Art **32**. And Art 131 is for
governments only — an option that lets a company or citizen file under 131 is wrong.

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 397 words**

Criminal law usually punishes a person for what he meant to do, judged on the facts as he understood them. Two sections of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 build on that idea. Section 14, which repeats section 76 of the old Penal Code, protects a person who is bound by law to do an act, or who, by reason of a mistake of fact and not of law, in good faith believes himself to be so bound. Section 17, which repeats section 79, gives the same protection to a person who is justified by law, or who by such a mistake believes himself to be justified.

The difference lies in a single word. "Bound" describes a duty: a court officer ordered to arrest a named person must act. "Justified" describes a permission: a passer-by who sees what looks like a murder and seizes the attacker to hand him to the police is allowed to act but is not required to. If the officer, after careful enquiry, arrests the wrong man, he is protected under section 14; if the attacker was in fact defending himself, the passer-by is protected under section 17.

Both sections carry two conditions. First, the mistake must be one of fact, not law. A man who knows exactly what he is doing but wrongly thinks the law allows it has made a mistake of law, and the old maxim applies: ignorance of the law excuses no one. In State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965), a passenger who carried gold through India was convicted even though he did not know of a notification issued only weeks earlier. Second, the belief must be held in good faith, which the Sanhita defines strictly: nothing is done in good faith if it is done without due care and attention. An honest belief formed carelessly does not qualify.

The facts, if true, must also have made the act lawful. In Chirangi v. State (1952), a man who, in a moment of delusion, struck his own son with an axe believing he was attacking a tiger was acquitted, because killing a tiger that threatened him would have been no offence. The courts looked at the world as he believed it to be, not as it was.

The sections are therefore narrow. They do not reward the reckless, and they never cover the person who simply misreads the law.

**1.** A court bailiff holds a warrant to arrest "Ravi Kumar of 12 Mill Road". At that address he compares the warrant photograph,
asks two neighbours, and arrests the man who answers to the name. The man is in fact Ravi's identical twin, Raghu. On the passage:
- (A) The bailiff has committed an offence, because he arrested the wrong person.
- (B) The bailiff is protected under section 17, because the law permitted him to arrest.
- (C) The bailiff is protected under section 14: he was bound to arrest, and his mistake about identity was one of fact, made with due care.
- (D) The bailiff is protected only if Raghu agrees not to complain.

**2.** Same warrant. This time the bailiff checks nothing and arrests the first man on Mill Road he hears someone call "Kumar". The man is
not Ravi. On the passage:
- (A) He is protected, because he honestly believed the man was Ravi.
- (B) He is not protected, because a belief formed without due care and attention is not held in good faith.
- (C) He is protected under section 17, because arrests under a warrant are permitted.
- (D) He is not protected, because a mistake about identity is a mistake of law.

**3.** Meena sees a man forcing a struggling woman into a car. She blocks the car and holds the man until the police arrive. It turns out
the woman was his sister, being taken to hospital during a seizure. Assuming Meena acted with due care, on the passage:
- (A) She is protected under section 14, because the law bound her to intervene.
- (B) She has no protection, because her belief turned out to be wrong.
- (C) She has no protection, because a private person can never detain anyone.
- (D) She is protected under section 17: by a mistake of fact, in good faith, she believed she was justified — permitted, not required — to act.

**4.** A landlord locks a tenant in a room until the rent is paid. He had read a tenancy statute and honestly concluded that it allowed
landlords to do this; it does not. On the passage:
- (A) He is not protected: he knew exactly what he was doing and was wrong only about what the law allows — a mistake of law.
- (B) He is protected, because he took care to read the statute, so his belief was in good faith.
- (C) He is protected under section 17, because he believed himself justified.
- (D) He is not protected, but only because he did not consult a lawyer.

**6.** *(LEG-PRIN)* **Principle:** A person in possession of a prohibited article is not guilty if, by reason of a mistake of fact made in
good faith, he did not know the article was in his possession. Ignorance of the law is no excuse.
**Facts:** At an airport carousel, Pavan picks up a black suitcase identical to his own after checking that it has the same size, the same
red ribbon and a tag in the same airline's colours. Customs finds prohibited drugs in it. The case is not his.
- (A) Not guilty: a good-faith mistake of fact about which suitcase was his meant he did not know the drugs were in his possession.
- (B) Guilty: possession is complete once the suitcase is in his hands.
- (C) Guilty: ignorance of the law is no excuse.
- (D) Not guilty, because a mistake of any kind removes guilt.

**7.** *(LEG-TORT review)* **Principle:** An employer is liable for a tort committed by an employee in the course of employment, which

**Facts:** QuickMart tells its delivery riders in writing never to jump traffic lights. Sunil, a QuickMart rider, jumps a red light to
