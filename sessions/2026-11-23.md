1. **A balance-sheet entry can restart the limitation clock (SC, *Yusuf v. Vigyapan Loke Delhi Advertising CGHS Ltd.*, 6 Nov 2026)** — *LEG-NEW.* A bench of
   Vikram Nath, Augustine George Masih and Sandeep Mehta JJ restored a money suit that had been thrown out as time-barred. The Court repeated that a debt shown
   in the debtor's balance sheet can be an **acknowledgment of liability**, which gives the creditor a fresh limitation period. *Hook:* **s. 18, Limitation Act,
   1963.** The acknowledgment must be **in writing**, **signed** by the debtor, and made **before the period runs out**. The new period runs from the date of the
   acknowledgment. *Likely Q:* "A written, signed acknowledgment of a debt gives a fresh period of limitation under which section?" (s. 18)

   permanent injunction against apps, domain-name holders and poster sellers using his name, image and voice commercially without permission, and extended it to
   unidentified infringers. *Hook:* India has **no single statute** on personality rights. Courts protect them through the **right to privacy and dignity under
   Art 21** (*Puttaswamy*, 2017), the tort of **passing off**, and **performers' rights** (s. 38, Copyright Act, 1957). *Likely Q:* "Personality rights in India
   rest mainly on…" (court-made law drawing on Art 21 and passing off, not a dedicated Act)

## Lesson — NLSAT current affairs: from headline to hook (≈ 6 min)

**What NLSAT does.** Part A has about 25 current-affairs and GK questions among 75. Many are set on a short news passage, and the passage usually gives you the
event. What it does not give you is the **static fact behind the event**: the Article, the section, the body and its parent ministry, the founding year.
That fact is where the marks are.

**The method: the HOOK LADDER.** For every news item, climb four rungs. Write one word on each rung in the margin.
1. **What happened**, with the date: one line, in your own words.
2. **Which law?** The Article, Act and section the event runs on. For criminal law, say both the old and the new code numbers.
3. **Which body?** Who acted, who it reports to, and where it sits (HQ, ministry, chair).
4. **Which number?** The one figure an examiner can turn into four options (rate, rank, count, year).

**Worked example (NLSAT style).** The headline is *"RBI raises repo rate by 25 bps to 5.50%; stance 'calibrated tightening' (9 Oct 2026)"*.
- **What:** the policy rate went up a quarter-point, to 5.50%.
- **Which law:** the **RBI Act, 1934**. Under **s. 45ZA** the Centre sets the inflation target, and **s. 45ZB** creates the Monetary Policy Committee.
- **Which body:** the **MPC** has 6 members (3 from the RBI, 3 appointed by the Centre) and is chaired by the Governor, who has a casting vote.
- **Which number:** the target is **4% CPI inflation, with a band of 2–6%**. Inflation has been running above the band (October CPI: 6.09%), which is why the stance turned to tightening.

A likely question: *"The inflation target the MPC must meet is set by: (A) the RBI Governor (B) the MPC itself (C) the Central Government, in consultation with the
RBI (D) the Finance Commission."* The answer is (C). The passage never tells you this; the ladder does.

**The traps examiners set**
- **The twin body.** RBI vs SEBI, CERT-In vs I4C, IEA vs IAEA. Ask which **ministry** each sits under; that usually separates them.
- **The old number.** A rate, a count or a rank that was true last year (the repo rate before the hike, "44 labour laws" before the Codes).
- **The host that isn't a member.** A summit held in a country is no proof that the country belongs to the group.

**NLSAT marking: a blank costs you.** NLSAT takes **−0.25 for a wrong answer *and* for an unanswered one**. A blank therefore always scores −0.25. A pure guess
among four options scores, on average, 0.25 × 1 − 0.75 × 0.25 = **+0.06**. So **never leave an NLSAT question blank.** At the very least, guess. (CLAT and AILET
are different: a blank there is 0. Guess only when you can strike out at least one option, which lifts the average to +0.17.)

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 9 again: Article 21, second pass (≈ 4 min)

Yesterday's capsule went unanswered, so Article 21 runs once more. This time the focus is on the rights the Court has **read into** Art 21.

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 21 protects every person**, citizen or foreigner. Since the 44th Amendment, Arts 20 and 21 **cannot be suspended** even in a National Emergency (Art 359).
2. ***Maneka Gandhi* (1978, 7 judges)** → the procedure must be **fair, just and reasonable**. It created the 14–19–21 "golden triangle", and every right below
   rests on it.
3. ***Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985, 5 judges)** → **right to livelihood**. Pavement dwellers could be evicted only after a fair
   procedure, with notice and a hearing.
4. ***Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979)** → **speedy trial** and free legal aid for undertrials. It links to Art 39A, a Directive Principle.
5. ***K. S. Puttaswamy* (2017, 9 judges)** → **privacy**. It is also the base for later rulings on data, dignity and personality rights (see brief item 2).

**Say-it drill.** Cover the lines above and say what each one did: **every person / 359 · Maneka · Olga Tellis · Hussainara · Puttaswamy**.

**Case:** ***D. K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997)** held that custodial violence violates Art 21. It laid down arrest guidelines: an arrest memo
witnessed and signed, a relative told, and a medical check every 48 hours. Most of these are now part of the arrest procedure in the BNSS.

**The trap:** *Olga Tellis* gave a right to livelihood and to a **fair procedure** before eviction. It did **not** give a right to stay on public pavements.
"Speedy trial" is *Hussainara*, not *Maneka*. And no right read into Art 21 is absolute: each can be restricted by a **fair law**.

The call usually begins with a uniform. A man dressed as a police or CBI officer appears on a video call and tells the victim that a parcel in her name has been seized with drugs, or that her Aadhaar number has been used to open accounts for money laundering. She is told she is under "digital arrest": she must stay on camera, tell no one, and transfer her savings to a "verification account" until her name is cleared. Over the past two years such calls have cost Indians thousands of crores, and the victims are disproportionately retired people.

Nothing in Indian law allows an arrest by video call. Arrest is a physical act governed by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure on 1 July 2024. An arrested person must be told the grounds of arrest and must be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours, a guarantee written into the Constitution itself. A "digital arrest" has none of these features, because it is not an arrest at all; it is an extortion script.

The criminal law already covers the conduct. Cheating by personation is an offence under section 319 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and the Information Technology Act, 2000 separately punishes cheating by personation using a computer resource. The harder problem is not defining the crime but recovering the money, which typically passes through a chain of "mule" accounts within minutes.

That is why the Supreme Court's attention in its suo motu proceedings on digital-arrest frauds has turned to banks. In October 2026 the Court indicated that financial institutions which fail in due diligence, allowing a pensioner's lifetime savings to move through newly opened accounts without a single check, may have to compensate victims, at least to the extent of the loss. It also urged safeguards such as temporarily holding suspicious transactions. The reasoning echoes a principle the Reserve Bank of India has applied since 2017: where an unauthorised electronic transaction results from a lapse in the bank's own system, or from a third-party breach that the customer reports promptly, the customer's liability is limited, in the best case to zero.

Critics say the approach places on banks a duty that belongs to the police. Supporters reply that banks are the only actors who see the money move in real time. Both sides agree that the first hour matters most: victims are advised to call the national cyber-crime helpline, 1930, so that the funds can be frozen before they scatter.
