# Day 2 — Contract: offer, acceptance and the timing of communication (CLAT Legal Reasoning)

## Lesson

**The concept.** A contract starts with a *proposal* (offer) and an *acceptance* that matches it exactly.
CLAT loves three questions here: (1) Was there an offer at all, or only an invitation to make one?
(2) Was the reply an acceptance, or a counter-offer? (3) Who communicated what, and *when* — did a
revocation arrive in time? Under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, acceptance must be absolute and
unqualified (s. 7), and the timing rules are in ss. 4–5.

**Rules of thumb.**
1. **Offer vs invitation to treat.** Goods displayed in a shop, catalogues, price lists, auction
   calls and tender notices *invite* offers. The customer makes the offer; the seller accepts or not.
2. **Counter-offer kills the original.** "I accept, but at ₹48 lakh" is a rejection plus a new offer.
   The original offer is dead and cannot be revived by a later "OK, I'll take your price".
   A mere *request for information* ("can I pay in instalments?") is not a counter-offer.
3. **No acceptance without knowledge.** You cannot accept an offer you did not know about
   (*Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt*, Allahabad HC, 1913: a servant who traced a missing boy before
   learning of the reward could not claim it).
4. **The timing table (s. 4–5) — learn it as two clocks.**

| Communication | Complete *against the sender* | Complete *against the receiver* |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance (sent by acceptor) | — | proposer: when **posted** (out of acceptor's power) · acceptor: when it **reaches** proposer |
| Revocation (by anyone) | when **posted** | when it **reaches** the other side |

   So: an **offer** can be revoked only until the acceptance is *posted*.
   An **acceptance** can be revoked until it *reaches* the proposer.
   A revocation works only if it *reaches* the other side before that deadline.

**Worked example (CLAT style).**
*Facts:* On 1 March Dev writes to Esha offering his printing press for ₹6 lakh. On 3 March Dev posts
a letter revoking the offer. On 4 March Esha posts her acceptance. Dev's revocation reaches Esha on
5 March; her acceptance reaches Dev on 6 March. Is there a contract?
*Working:* Deadline to revoke the offer = when Esha's acceptance was posted = 4 March. Dev's revocation
is effective against Esha only when it reached her = 5 March — one day too late. Posting it on
3 March is irrelevant (that only binds Dev). **Contract formed.**

**The traps.**
- *"He posted the revocation first."* The date of posting a revocation binds the sender, not the receiver.
- *"The acceptance hadn't arrived yet, so the offer was still open."* For the proposer, acceptance is
  complete on posting.
- *Mistaken price in the window.* The answer is usually "no contract — invitation to treat", not
  "no contract — mistake". Grade the *because*.
- *Eventual agreement after a counter-offer.* Feels like a deal; legally it is a fresh offer.

## Test — 8 questions, ~10 minutes, +1 / −0.25

### Passage I (Q1–4)

A contract arises only when one person makes a proposal and another accepts it. Not every statement
about price is a proposal. A shopkeeper who displays goods with price tags, or a company that
circulates a price list, is ordinarily inviting customers to make offers; it is the customer who
proposes to buy, and the seller remains free to accept or refuse. Only when the seller agrees does a
contract come into being.

An acceptance must mirror the proposal. A reply that accepts on altered terms is not an acceptance at
all; it is a rejection of the original proposal coupled with a fresh proposal of its own. Once
rejected in this way, the original proposal lapses and cannot afterwards be accepted, unless the
person who made it chooses to renew it. A reply that merely asks a question about the proposal,
without rejecting it or putting forward different terms, is treated differently: the proposal
survives the question and may still be accepted while it remains open.

Finally, acceptance is a conscious act. A person who does what a proposal asks, but does so without
knowing that the proposal exists, has not accepted it, and cannot later rely on it — even if he
learns of the proposal immediately afterwards.

**1.** A mobile shop displays a phone in its window with a tag reading "₹9,999". The correct price is
₹99,999; a staff member mistyped the tag. Neha walks in, places ₹9,999 on the counter and asks for the
phone. The owner refuses. Neha claims a contract was formed. Decide.
- (A) A contract was formed, since the window display was a proposal that Neha accepted by tendering the price.
- (B) No contract was formed, since a contract cannot arise from a price that was entered by mistake.
- (C) No contract was formed, since the display only invited offers; Neha made the offer, which the owner was free to refuse.
- (D) A contract was formed, since a shop is bound to sell at the price it displays.

**2.** Ravi writes to Sana offering to sell his plot for ₹50 lakh. Sana replies: "I accept, at ₹48 lakh."
Ravi does not respond. A week later Sana writes: "Very well, I accept ₹50 lakh." Ravi refuses to sell.
Decide.
- (A) No contract: Sana's first reply rejected Ravi's proposal, and her second letter was only a fresh proposal that Ravi could refuse.
- (B) A contract at ₹50 lakh: Sana accepted Ravi's exact terms, and Ravi had never withdrawn his proposal.
- (C) No contract: Ravi's silence for a week amounted to withdrawal of his proposal.
- (D) A contract at ₹48 lakh: Ravi did not object to Sana's figure.

**3.** Same offer by Ravi. This time Sana replies: "Would you accept the ₹50 lakh in two instalments?"
Ravi does not answer. Two days later, before Ravi has withdrawn his proposal, Sana writes: "I accept
your offer of ₹50 lakh, payable in full." Ravi refuses to sell. Decide.
- (A) No contract: Sana's question altered the terms and so rejected Ravi's proposal.
- (B) No contract: Ravi never agreed to instalments, so the parties never agreed on terms.
- (C) A contract on instalment terms: Ravi's failure to reply amounted to consent.
- (D) A contract at ₹50 lakh: Sana's question did not reject the proposal, which remained open and was then accepted unconditionally.

**4.** Kavya announces in a local newspaper a reward of ₹25,000 for the return of her lost dog. Tarun,
who does not read newspapers, finds the dog, sees Kavya's address on its collar and returns it. On his
way home he sees the advertisement and claims the reward. Decide.
- (A) Tarun is entitled: he did exactly what the proposal asked, and learnt of it on the same day.
- (B) Tarun is not entitled: he returned the dog without knowing of the proposal, so he did not accept it.
- (C) Tarun is entitled: a reward offered to the public can be accepted by anyone who performs the act.
- (D) Tarun is not entitled: a newspaper advertisement is only an invitation to treat.

### Passage II (Q5–8)

When parties deal at a distance, the law must decide when each communication takes effect. The
communication of an acceptance is complete as against the proposer when the acceptance is put in the
course of transmission to him, so that it is out of the acceptor's power to recall it; it is complete
as against the acceptor only when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer.

A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as
against the proposer, but not afterwards. An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the
communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards.

The communication of a revocation is complete as against the person who makes it when it is put in
the course of transmission, and as against the person to whom it is made only when it comes to that
person's knowledge. A revocation therefore takes effect against the other party only when it reaches
him.

*(Facts for Q5–7, each question independent.)* On 1 June, Anil, in Delhi, posts a letter to Bina, in
Pune, offering to sell her his car for ₹4 lakh.

**5.** Bina posts her acceptance on 5 June; it reaches Anil on 8 June. Anil, having changed his mind,
posted a letter of revocation on 4 June, which reached Bina on 6 June. Is there a contract?
- (A) Yes: the acceptance was complete against Anil on 5 June, and his revocation reached Bina only on 6 June, after the deadline for revoking his proposal.
- (B) No: Anil posted his revocation on 4 June, before Bina posted her acceptance.
- (C) No: Anil's revocation reached Bina on 6 June, before her acceptance reached Anil on 8 June.
- (D) Yes: a proposal sent by post cannot be revoked once it has reached the other party.

**6.** On the morning of 5 June, before Bina posts her acceptance, Anil telephones her and says he
withdraws his offer. Bina posts her acceptance that afternoon anyway. Is there a contract?
- (A) Yes: a written proposal can only be revoked in writing.
- (B) Yes: Bina's acceptance was complete against Anil the moment she posted it.
- (C) No: the revocation came to Bina's knowledge before her acceptance was put in the course of transmission.
- (D) No: an acceptance sent after a telephone conversation is invalid.

**7.** Bina posts her acceptance on 5 June; it reaches Anil on 8 June. On 6 June Bina changes her mind
and sends Anil an email revoking her acceptance, which Anil reads on 7 June. Is there a contract?
- (A) Yes: the acceptance was complete against Anil when posted on 5 June, so it could no longer be revoked.
- (B) Yes: an acceptance, once posted, is out of the acceptor's power and cannot be recalled.
- (C) No: Bina changed her mind before her acceptance reached Anil, and that is enough.
- (D) No: the acceptance was complete against Bina only on 8 June, and her revocation came to Anil's knowledge on 7 June, before that.

**8.** In a dispute over whether a proposer's revocation was effective, which fact is the most relevant
under the passage?
- (A) The date on which the proposer wrote and posted the revocation.
- (B) The date on which the revocation came to the acceptor's knowledge, compared with the date on which the acceptor put the acceptance in the course of transmission.
- (C) The date on which the acceptance reached the proposer.
- (D) Whether the proposer had a good reason for changing his mind.
