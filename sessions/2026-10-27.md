# Day 8 — Principle application, level 2: many principles, one deciding fact (NLSAT Part A)

## Lesson

**Why this, today.** You went 9/9 on single-principle questions in Days 1–2. NLSAT's reasoning section
(and its Part B) is harder in one specific way: it gives you a *set* of principles — a rule, a
qualifier, an exception — and the question turns on which one the facts trigger. People who are good
at single principles lose marks here by applying the right rule to the wrong fact.

**The method — "Number, Map, Qualify":**
1. **Number the principles** (P1, P2, P3…) as you read. Each usually adds one element or one exception.
2. **Map each fact to one principle.** Ask of every fact in the problem: *which numbered principle is
   this fact here to trigger?* A fact that maps to nothing is a distractor.
3. **Qualify.** Circle limiting words: *only*, *to the extent*, *at any time*, *as easy as*, *before*,
   *unless*. Exam answers are built from these. "Invalid **to the extent** that…" means partly valid,
   not void.
4. **Choose the option with the right result *and* the right reason.** Two options often reach the
   same result; only one uses the principle that actually decides it.

**Real-world anchor.** The Ministry of Electronics and IT notified the **Digital Personal Data Protection
Rules, 2025 on 14 November 2025**, operationalising the DPDP Act, 2023; most obligations (notices,
consent managers, core processing rules) phase in over 12–18 months, and the Act allows penalties of up
to ₹250 crore per breach, imposed by the Data Protection Board. The Act's consent standard — consent must
be *free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, by a clear affirmative action, and limited to
data necessary for the purpose* — is exactly the kind of multi-element principle NLSAT builds passages on.
Expect it in CA and legal reasoning this cycle.

**Worked example (NLSAT style).**
*Principles:* P1 — Consent to use personal data is valid only if given by a clear affirmative action.
P2 — Where consent is sought for several purposes together, it is invalid to the extent it covers data
not necessary for the purpose for which the service is used.
*Facts:* A ride-hailing app asks Dev to tap "Agree" to (a) use his location to send a cab and (b) read
his SMS inbox "to improve services". He taps Agree and books a ride.
*Which is correct?*
(A) Consent is wholly invalid because it bundled two purposes. (B) Consent is valid for both, since Dev tapped Agree.
(C) Consent is valid for location, invalid for the SMS inbox. (D) Consent is invalid because Dev did not read the terms.
**Answer: (C).** Number: P1 (affirmative action) is satisfied — he tapped. Map: the SMS fact maps to P2.
Qualify: "invalid **to the extent**" → only the unnecessary part fails. (A) is the trap — right instinct,
wrong scope. (D) imports a requirement the principles don't contain.

**The trap examiners set.** Importing what you "know" or feel is fair. If the principle says
"to the extent", don't void the whole thing; if it says the right lasts "only as long as the danger
lasts", don't protect what happens after the danger is over — however sympathetic the person.

**One NLSAT-specific rule to remember:** in Part A an unanswered question costs −0.25, the same as a
wrong one. Never leave a question blank — eliminate and commit.

## Test — 8 questions, NLSAT Part A pattern (+1 / −0.25 for wrong *or* unanswered). Aim ≤ 8 minutes.

Apply only the principles given, even if you believe the real law differs.

### Passage I — Q1–4

A growing share of daily life now runs through apps, and with it a quiet trade: services in return for
personal information. Lawmakers have tried to make that trade fairer not by banning it but by insisting
that the person whose data is used should actually choose to part with it. Assume the following
principles govern a "data fiduciary" (any business that decides why and how personal data is used):

**P1.** Consent to the use of personal data is valid only if it is free, specific, informed, unconditional
and unambiguous, and is given by a clear affirmative action of the person concerned.
**P2.** Where a single request for consent covers data that is not necessary for the purpose for which
the person uses the service, the consent is invalid to the extent of the unnecessary data.
**P3.** A person may withdraw consent at any time, and the means of withdrawing must be as easy as the
means by which consent was given. Withdrawal does not make unlawful any use of the data made before it.
**P4.** Without asking for consent, a fiduciary may use personal data that the person voluntarily provided
to it for a specified purpose, but only for that purpose, and only if the person has not indicated that
she does not consent to that use.

The architects of such rules concede that they ask more of businesses than a click-through screen ever
did. Their reply is that a choice the user never notices making is not a choice at all.

**1.** Riya signs up for a food-delivery app. The sign-up screen contains a box, already ticked, reading
"I agree to share my contacts and location history with partners for marketing." Riya does not untick it
and completes sign-up. The app shares her contacts with a partner. Under the principles, which is correct?
- (A) The consent is valid, because Riya could have unticked the box and chose not to.
- (B) The consent is valid, because sharing data with partners is common practice in the industry.
- (C) The consent is not valid, because a pre-ticked box that Riya merely left alone is not a clear affirmative action by her.
- (D) The consent is not valid, because a food-delivery app may never share personal data with anyone.

**2.** A pharmacy app requires users to press a single "I agree" button before ordering. The button covers
the user's name, address and prescription (to deliver the medicine) and access to the user's photo
gallery. Kabir presses it and orders medicine. Which is correct?
- (A) Kabir's consent is valid for his name, address and prescription, but invalid for his photo gallery.
- (B) Kabir's consent is wholly invalid, because it bundled necessary and unnecessary data in one request.
- (C) Kabir's consent is wholly valid, because he pressed the button himself.
- (D) Kabir's consent is invalid for his prescription, because health data can never be shared with a business.

**3.** Meena gave consent to a fitness app with a single tap. To withdraw, the app requires a signed letter
with a notarised copy of an identity document, sent by post. In February the app used her data to send
her promotions; on 1 March she posts the withdrawal letter. Which statement is most accurate?
- (A) The February use was unlawful, because Meena has now withdrawn her consent.
- (B) The app's withdrawal procedure is acceptable, because it protects Meena against fraudulent withdrawals.
- (C) Meena cannot withdraw consent once the app has already used her data.
- (D) The February use remains lawful, but the app's withdrawal procedure does not satisfy the principles.

**4.** Arjun e-mails a bookshop asking for the price of a rare atlas and adds, "Please call me on this number
with the price." The shop calls him with the price. A week later it adds his number to its WhatsApp group
for promotional offers. Neither use was preceded by a request for consent. Which is correct?
- (A) Both uses are lawful, because Arjun voluntarily gave the shop his number.
- (B) The call was lawful without consent; adding him to the promotional group was not.
- (C) Both uses are unlawful, because the shop never asked for consent.
- (D) The call was unlawful, but the promotional group was lawful, because Arjun is a customer.

### Passage II — Q5–6

Assume the following principles, modelled on the general exceptions in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023:

**P1.** Nothing is an offence that is done in the exercise of the right of private defence. Every person
has a right to defend his own body, and the body of any other person, against any offence affecting the
human body.
**P2.** The right begins as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt
or threat to commit the offence, and it continues only as long as that apprehension continues.
**P3.** There is no right of private defence where there is time to seek the protection of the public
authorities, and the right never extends to causing more harm than is necessary for the purpose of defence.

**5.** Late at night Harsh swings an iron rod at Dinesh's head in a parking lot. Dinesh shoves Harsh hard;
Harsh falls, hits his head on the kerb and lies unconscious. Dinesh, still furious, then kicks him
several times in the ribs, breaking two of them. Which is correct?
- (A) Dinesh is protected for everything he did, because Harsh started the attack with a dangerous weapon.
- (B) Dinesh is protected for nothing, because he should have called the police instead of shoving Harsh.
- (C) Dinesh is protected for the shove but not for the kicks, because the apprehension of danger ended once Harsh lay unconscious.
- (D) Dinesh is not protected for the shove, because it caused Harsh a head injury.

**6.** On Monday, Imran phones Farhan and says, "Next Sunday I will come to your house and break your legs."
On Tuesday morning, Farhan goes to Imran's office and hits him with a cricket bat, saying he was only
defending himself in advance. Which is correct?
- (A) Farhan has no right of private defence: there was time to seek the protection of the public authorities, and no danger to his body was then imminent.
- (B) Farhan has a right of private defence, because the threat created a reasonable apprehension of danger.
- (C) Farhan has a right of private defence, but he caused more harm than necessary.
- (D) Farhan has no right of private defence, because a threat made over the phone is never an offence affecting the body.

### Passage III — Q7–8

Assume the following principles of the Indian Contract Act, 1872:
The communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is
made. The communication of an acceptance is complete — as against the proposer, when it is put in a course
of transmission to him so as to be out of the power of the acceptor; as against the acceptor, when it comes
to the knowledge of the proposer. A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its
acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards. An acceptance may be revoked at any time
before the communication of the acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards. The
communication of a revocation is complete, as against the person to whom it is made, when it comes to his
knowledge.

**7.** On 1 October Asha posts an offer to sell her piano to Bimal; it reaches him on 3 October. On
3 October Asha posts a letter revoking the offer; it reaches Bimal on 5 October. On 4 October Bimal posts
his acceptance, which reaches Asha on 7 October. Which is correct?
- (A) There is no contract, because Asha posted her revocation on 3 October, before Bimal posted his acceptance.
- (B) There is no contract, because Asha's revocation reached Bimal (5 October) before his acceptance reached Asha (7 October).
- (C) There is a contract, but only from 7 October, when the acceptance reached Asha.
- (D) There is a contract, because the acceptance was complete against Asha when posted on 4 October, before her revocation came to Bimal's knowledge on 5 October.

**8.** Change the facts: there is no revocation by Asha. Bimal posts his acceptance on 4 October; it reaches
Asha on 7 October. On 5 October Bimal changes his mind and sends a revocation of his acceptance by courier,
which Asha receives on 6 October. Which is correct?
- (A) There is a contract, because the acceptance was complete as soon as Bimal posted it on 4 October.
- (B) There is no contract, because Bimal's revocation reached Asha before his acceptance came to her knowledge.
- (C) There is no contract, because an acceptance, once posted, can be revoked at any time.
- (D) There is a contract, because a revocation sent by courier is not a valid mode of communication.

## Answer key (do not send)

| Q | Ans | Tag | Why | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C | LEG-PRIN | P1: consent needs a clear affirmative act; a pre-ticked box left alone is inaction. | (A) "could have unticked" = silence as consent; (D) overstates — sharing is allowed with valid consent. |
| 2 | A | LEG-PRIN | P2: invalid **to the extent** of unnecessary data (gallery); valid for delivery data. | (B) voids the whole consent — ignores "to the extent"; (D) imports an outside rule. |
| 3 | D | LEG-PRIN | P3: pre-withdrawal use stays lawful; withdrawal must be as easy as giving (one tap vs notarised post). | (A) ignores P3's second sentence; (B) a policy reason the principle doesn't allow. |
| 4 | B | LEG-PRIN | P4: voluntarily given for a specified purpose (price call) → lawful for that purpose only; promotions = new purpose needing consent. | (A) "voluntarily gave" stretched beyond the specified purpose. |
| 5 | C | LEG-PRIN | P2: right lasts only while apprehension lasts; unconscious attacker = no danger → kicks unprotected. The shove was proportionate. | (A) sympathy for victim; (D) result of a proportionate act doesn't make it excessive. |
| 6 | A | LEG-PRIN | P3: a week's notice = time to approach police; P2: no present apprehension. | (B) apprehension of a future attack doesn't authorise a pre-emptive strike when authorities are available; (D) wrong reason (a threat can trigger the right if imminent). |
| 7 | D | LEG-CONT | Acceptance complete against proposer on posting (4 Oct); revocation needed to reach Bimal before that; it reached 5 Oct → too late. | (A) uses the posting date of the revocation — exactly the Day-2 Q5 error; (B) compares with the wrong clock. |
| 8 | B | LEG-CONT | Acceptance complete against acceptor only when it reaches proposer (7 Oct); revocation reached 6 Oct → validly revoked. | (A) uses the proposer's clock for the acceptor; (C) overstated ("any time"). |
