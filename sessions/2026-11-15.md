## Lesson — The CLAT current-affairs passage: read for the static nouns (≈ 6 min)

**How the section works.** CLAT's current-affairs section is 5–6 passages of about 450 words, each a news story, with 5–6 questions per passage. The
twist: **most questions are not answered by the passage.** The story is only the hook; the question asks the static fact sitting behind a noun in it —
the body's headquarters, the Article, the first holder, the next host. Some names are blanked out as [1], [2] and you must fill them in.

**Method — the noun sweep (≈ 40 seconds before the questions).**
1. **Read once at speed for the story** (what happened, where, when) — no stopping.
2. **Box every "static noun"**: a body (FIDE, ISA, UNFCCC), a place, a law or Article, a title/rank ("first", "100th", "youngest"), a number.
   Each box is a likely question.
3. **For each box, recall the triad**: *HQ · head · founded/first*. For a law: *Article/section · year · the case*.
4. **Blanks [1], [2]:** use the clues around the blank (year, role, country) — they usually point to one name only.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** A passage line reads: *"COP31 opened in Antalya on 9 November, with Türkiye as host and Australia leading the
negotiations under the Convention."* The question: *"The secretariat of the Convention referred to is located in — (a) Geneva (b) Bonn (c) Nairobi
(d) New York."* The passage never mentions a secretariat. The noun sweep boxes "the Convention" → UNFCCC → triad: **HQ Bonn**, adopted at Rio 1992,
COP = its annual meeting. **Answer (b).** Geneva (WMO, WHO) and Nairobi (UNEP) are the look-alike UN cities examiners love.

**The trap.** (1) **The passage's own numbers as distractors** — an option repeats a figure from the story (a rating, a year) for a different question;
check *which* fact the stem asks for. (2) **Superlatives**: "first", "youngest", "only" — the passage may say "first from Madhya Pradesh", the option
says "first from India". (3) **Founding city ≠ HQ** (FIDE was founded in Paris; it is not based there).

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 5 repeat: The Fundamental Rights map (≈ 4 min)

*(Repeated because the 14 Nov capsule went unanswered. Same unit, new angle — read it as a recall check: try to say each fact before you read it.)*

**Five flash facts**
1. **Part III = Arts 12–35.** **Art 12** "State" includes "other authorities" — a body under the government's **deep and pervasive control** counts.
2. **Art 13** → any law that takes away a Fundamental Right is **void to the extent** of the conflict; "law" includes ordinances, rules and customs.
3. **Six groups** → Equality **14–18** · Freedom **19–22** · Against exploitation **23–24** · Religion **25–28** · Cultural & educational **29–30** · Remedies **32**.
4. **Property** → the **44th Amendment (1978)** deleted **19(1)(f)** and **31**; property is now **Art 300A** (Part XII) — a constitutional right, enforced in the
   **High Court (Art 226)**, not via Art 32.
5. **Citizens only** → **15, 16, 19, 29, 30** (memory peg: "15-16, 19, 29-30"). Everyone, foreigners included → **14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 25**.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts: **12 · 13 · 14–18 / 19–22 / 23–24 / 25–28 / 29–30 / 32 · 44th → 300A · 15-16-19-29-30.**

**Case.** *Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology* (2002, **7 judges**): a body is "State" under Art 12 if it is financially,
functionally and administratively under the government's **deep and pervasive control** — CSIR was held to be State (refining *Ajay Hasia*, 1981).

**The trap.** "Art 21 is for citizens only" — wrong: **Art 21 protects every person**. And the citizens-only list never includes **14** (equality before
law is for "any person").

**Passage 1 (Questions 1–5) — 445 words**

On 8 November 2026, a 22-year-old International Master from Katni in Madhya Pradesh sat down for the last round in Ozamiz City, in the southern Philippines, knowing that a good result would make history. By the evening, Anuj Shrivatri had won the 7th ASEAN+ Individual Open Chess Championship with 7 points from 9 rounds, half a point clear of Vietnam's Grandmaster Nguyen Duc Hoa. Under the event's special format, the winner receives the Grandmaster title directly. Shrivatri thus became India's 100th Grandmaster, and the first ever from Madhya Pradesh.

The road was not smooth. He lost his opening game and had to win most of the rest to stay in contention. Off the board his story is unusual too: he studied computer science, took a job as a software engineer, and kept playing on the side, working largely on his own rather than at a big academy.

The title itself is awarded by the world chess body, FIDE. The usual route is long. A player must earn three Grandmaster norms — strong performances against tough fields — spread over at least 27 games, and must also cross a rating of 2,500. Shrivatri's rating after the win stood at about 2,450, which is why the direct-title route mattered so much to him.

India's Grandmaster story began in 1988, when [1] became the country's first. The second, Dibyendu Barua, followed only in 1991. For years the count grew slowly: India had 32 Grandmasters by 2013 and 50 by 2017. Then the numbers took off, with Tamil Nadu producing the largest share. Success at the top has fed the base below it. In December 2024, [2] became the youngest undisputed world champion in history after defeating China's Ding Liren in Singapore. The same year India won both the open and the women's gold medals at the Chess Olympiad in Budapest. And in 2025, the 19-year-old Divya Deshmukh earned the Grandmaster title in a single stroke by winning the FIDE Women's World Cup, beating Koneru Humpy in an all-Indian final.

The All India Chess Federation greeted the hundredth title with a short message: onwards to two hundred. Coaches, however, warn that numbers are not everything. Most of India's Grandmasters still come from a handful of cities, and players from smaller towns, like Shrivatri, often lack sponsors, trainers and a steady calendar of strong tournaments close to home. A title won in a distant tournament, after years of playing alongside a full-time job, says as much about the gaps in the system as about the talent it has produced. If the next hundred are to come faster, the coaches argue, they will have to come from places like Katni too.
