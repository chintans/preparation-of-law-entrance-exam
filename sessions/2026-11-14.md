## Lesson — Did X really cause Y? (≈ 8 min)

**The idea.** A large share of NLSAT and CLAT reasoning items are **causal arguments**: *"After X was introduced, Y happened — so X works, and
we should do more X."* Weaken and strengthen questions test one thing: whether you can see the **gap between "Y followed X" and "X caused Y"**.

**Three ways the link breaks (memorise as A-R-M):**
- **A — Another cause.** Something else changed at the same time (a fee rise, a new law, a festival season, a recession).
- **R — Reverse or selection.** Y caused X, or the people who got X were different to begin with (only toppers joined the club).
- **M — Mis-measured result.** The number fell, but not for the reason claimed: cases filed fell because they were *delayed*, crimes reported fell
  because people stopped *reporting*.

**Method — four steps, about 40 seconds.**
1. **Find the conclusion** — usually the recommendation ("should", "therefore", "every State must").
2. **Name the evidence** in five words ("suits fell 38% after rule").
3. **State the gap** in one line ("assumes the rule caused the fall, and the fall means settlement").
4. **Weaken = an option that opens the gap (A, R or M). Strengthen = an option that closes it** (rules out another cause; shows the result is real).
   Ignore options that attack a side point, repeat what the author already conceded, or bring in values the argument never used.

**Worked example (NLSAT style), from this month's news.** On 4 Nov 2026 the Supreme Court said laws disqualifying panchayat members with more than
two children have "lost relevance" and referred the precedent to a larger bench (Brief, 13 Nov). Suppose an editorial argues: *"NFHS-5 (2019–21) put
India's total fertility rate at 2.0, below the replacement level of 2.1. So two-child disqualification laws have outlived their purpose in every
State."* Which **weakens** it most?
- (a) The laws were first passed in the 1990s. — side point.
- (b) Some argue such laws are unfair to women. — a value the argument never used.
- (c) **In some States with such laws, fertility is still well above replacement (Bihar's NFHS-5 figure was about 3.0).** — opens the gap: a *national*
  average is used to prove a claim about *every State* (a measurement gap, M). **Answer (c).**
- (d) The Supreme Court has referred the matter to a larger bench. — restates the background; says nothing about the reasoning.

**The trap.** (1) The **conceded objection**: if the author already says "this is a fair concern, but…", an option repeating that concern barely
weakens. (2) **Premise attacks**: NLSAT treats the stated facts as true — the right weakener accepts the numbers and breaks the *link*. (3) On
NLSAT, a skipped question also costs **−0.25**, so eliminate two options and pick — never leave a blank.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 5: The Fundamental Rights map (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Part III, Arts 12–35** → the Fundamental Rights. **Art 12** defines "**State**" (Government and Parliament of India, State Governments and
   Legislatures, local and **other authorities**) — e.g. National Book Trust held "State" on 6 Nov 2026.
2. **Art 13** → laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are **void to the extent** of the inconsistency — the textual root of judicial review.
3. **Six groups** → Equality **14–18** · Freedom **19–22** · Against exploitation **23–24** · Religion **25–28** · Cultural & educational **29–30** ·
   Constitutional remedies **32** (Ambedkar: the "heart and soul" of the Constitution).
4. **Right to property** → was the seventh group (**Art 19(1)(f) and Art 31**); deleted by the **44th Amendment, 1978**; now **Art 300A** in **Part XII** —
   a constitutional (legal) right, not a Fundamental Right.
5. **Citizens only** → **Arts 15, 16, 19, 29, 30**. Everyone (citizens and foreigners) → e.g. **14, 20, 21, 22**. **Art 32** is itself a Fundamental Right.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts: **12 · 13 · 14–18 / 19–22 / 23–24 / 25–28 / 29–30 / 32 · 44th → 300A · 15-16-19-29-30.** Say each aloud, then check.

**Case.** *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi* (1981, **5 judges**): a society or body that is an "instrumentality or agency" of the government —
judged by funding, control and functions — is an "other authority", and so "State" under Art 12.

**The trap.** "**Seven** Fundamental Rights" is the 1950 answer — today there are **six**. And **Art 300A is not in Part III**: violating it lets you go to
the High Court under Art 226, **not** to the Supreme Court under Art 32.

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 400 words**

Two years ago, the State of Varunagar made mediation compulsory before any suit for the partition of family property could be filed in its civil courts. Parties now have to attend at least two sessions at a court-annexed mediation centre and obtain a certificate before a court will register the plaint. Critics warned that the rule would merely add one more delay at the courthouse door. The numbers suggest otherwise. In the two years since, fresh partition suits filed in Varunagar's courts have fallen by 38 per cent, and the State's civil pendency, which had risen every year for a decade, has declined for the first time.

The lesson seems plain: compulsory mediation works, and every State should make it a condition for filing family property disputes. When relatives sit across a table with a trained mediator instead of facing each other through lawyers, they often discover that a quarrel over land is really a quarrel about respect, fairness and old grievances. A court can divide a field; only a conversation can settle a family. A settlement that both sides have shaped is also more likely to be honoured than a decree imposed by a judge, which is why fewer such disputes return to court years later as execution petitions.

Those who still oppose the rule make two arguments. The first is that compulsion defeats the very spirit of mediation, which depends on willingness. But willingness usually arrives only once the parties are in the room; very few would walk in on their own. The second is that the poorer or weaker party, often a sister claiming her share of the family land, may be pressured into accepting a settlement worth less than what the law would give her. This is a fair concern, and it deserves an answer. But it argues for better-trained mediators and a judge's review of every settlement, not for abandoning a scheme that is plainly working.

Varunagar has shown the way. With civil courts across the country carrying lakhs of pending property cases, and with litigants waiting a decade or more for a final decree, other States have no reason to wait for further proof. The cost of a mediation centre is small; the cost of another generation of families locked in litigation is not. The real question is not whether to follow Varunagar, but how quickly the rest of the country can do so.
