## Lesson — Common intention vs common object (≈ 6 min)

**The concept.** When a crime is done by a group, CLAT asks whether a person who did not strike the blow still answers for it. Two rules decide this,
and their numbering changed in 2024:

- **Common intention — BNS s. 3(5)** (old IPC s. 34): several people do a criminal act *in furtherance of a shared plan* → each is liable as if he did it
  alone. Needs (i) a **prior meeting of minds** (it can form on the spot, seconds before) and (ii) **participation** in some form — keeping watch counts.
- **Common object — BNS s. 190** (old IPC s. 149): an offence by any member of an **unlawful assembly (5 or more — BNS s. 189 / IPC s. 141)** in
  prosecution of its common object, or one members knew was likely → **every member at the time** is guilty. No plan, no individual act need be proved:
  **membership is enough**.

**Method — three questions, in this order.**
1. **How many people?** Fewer than five → s. 190 is out; only s. 3(5) is possible.
2. **Was there a shared plan, and did this person take part?** Both yes → s. 3(5). Same goal held separately (two strangers who each hate the victim) → no.
3. **For s. 190: was the offence within the object or known to be likely?** A frolic of one member outside the object binds only him.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *Principle:* as in s. 3(5) above. *Facts:* P and Q agree to break into a godown at night. Q stays outside on the road
to whistle if a patrol comes; P goes in and breaks the lock. Is Q liable for the house-breaking?
→ Shared plan ✓; Q took part by keeping watch ✓ → **liable as if he had broken in himself.** The option "No, because Q never entered the godown" is the
trap. (This is the old Privy Council line in *Barendra Kumar Ghosh* (1925): "they also serve who only stand and wait". *Mahbub Shah* (1945) adds the other
half — common intention means a pre-arranged plan, not merely similar intentions.)

**The traps examiners set.**
- **"Same" ≠ "common" intention.** Two people with the same aim, acting independently, are each liable only for their own act.
- **Presence ≠ participation** for s. 3(5); but **membership is enough** for s. 190 — don't carry one rule's test into the other.
- **Count heads.** Four people can never be an unlawful assembly. An option that applies s. 190 to four is wrong however neat its reasoning.
- **"At the time."** Joining after the offence, or leaving before it, takes a person outside s. 190.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 8: Articles 20–22 (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 20(1)** → no *ex post facto* criminal law: no conviction for an act that was not an offence when done, and no penalty heavier than the one in force
   then. → Applies only to **criminal** law (not tax or civil liability, not procedure).
2. **Art 20(2)** → **double jeopardy**: no one shall be **prosecuted and punished** for the same offence more than once. → Needs a prior prosecution *and*
   punishment before a court; **departmental proceedings are not "prosecution"** (*Maqbool Hussain*, 1953 — customs confiscation, then a criminal case, allowed).
3. **Art 20(3)** → no person **accused** of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. → *Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961, 11 judges): fingerprints and
   specimen handwriting are not "testimony"; *Selvi* (2010): narco, polygraph and brain-mapping tests without consent violate 20(3) and 21.
4. **Art 22(1)–(2)** → arrested person: told the grounds as soon as may be, may consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice, **produced before the nearest
   magistrate within 24 hours** (journey time excluded). → Not available to enemy aliens or to preventive detainees (22(3)).
5. **Art 22(4)–(7)** → **preventive detention**: no detention beyond **3 months** unless an **Advisory Board** reports sufficient cause; grounds must be
   communicated (22(5)), but facts can be withheld in the public interest (22(6)). → The 44th Am. would cut this to 2 months — that change was **never brought into force**.

**Say-it drill** — cover the lines above and say what each does: **20(1) · 20(2) · 20(3) · 22(2) · 22(4)**.

**Case:** ***Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010, 3 judges, CJI K. G. Balakrishnan)** — involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and BEAP tests are unconstitutional;
results of a voluntary test are not themselves evidence, but things discovered because of it can be (Evidence Act s. 27 → **BSA s. 23(2) proviso**).

**The trap:** Arts 20 and 21 **cannot be suspended even in an emergency** (Art 359, after the 44th Am.) — and Art 20 protects **every person**, not just citizens.
Art 20(2) needs *prosecuted and punished*: an acquittal followed by a retrial is a CrPC s. 300 / **BNSS s. 337** question, not Art 20(2).

**Passage 1 (Questions 1–5) — 432 words**

When several people commit a crime together, the criminal law must decide how far each of them answers for what the others did. Indian law gives two answers, and examiners like to set them side by side.

The first is common intention. Under section 3(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which carries forward section 34 of the old Penal Code, when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of them is liable for that act as if he had done it alone. Two things must be shown. There must be a prior meeting of minds, a shared plan, though the plan need not be formed long in advance; it can arise on the spot, in the moments before the act. And each accused must take part in the act in some way. Taking part need not mean striking the blow: the man who keeps watch at the gate, or waits in the getaway car, takes part. Mere presence at the scene, without a shared plan, is not enough. Nor is it enough that each person separately held the same intention; the intention must be common, not merely similar.

The second is common object. Under section 190 of the Sanhita (formerly section 149), if an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or is one that the members knew was likely to be committed in prosecuting that object, every person who was a member of the assembly at the time is guilty of that offence. An unlawful assembly needs five or more persons whose common object is one of those the statute lists, such as using criminal force or resisting the execution of a law. Here no prior meeting of minds need be proved and no individual act need be shown: membership of the assembly when the offence is committed is enough. The liability has a limit, though. If one member commits an offence that was neither part of the common object nor known to be a likely result of pursuing it, the others are not answerable for it.

The difference matters most in close cases. Section 3(5) can apply to just two people; section 190 needs at least five. Section 3(5) punishes a shared plan put into action; section 190 punishes membership of a group with a shared object. A person who joins the group only after the offence is over, or who left it before, was not a member "at the time", and section 190 cannot reach him.

**1.** A and B agree to give C a beating. A beats C inside C's shop while B stands at the shop door, turning customers away so that no one interferes.
Applying the passage, B is:
- (A) not liable for the beating, because he never touched C
- (B) liable for the beating as if he had done it alone, because there was a shared plan and he took part by guarding the door
- (C) liable for the beating under section 190, because he was a member of an unlawful assembly
- (D) liable only if C proves that B suggested the beating

**2.** X and Y, who do not know each other, both bear a grudge against Z. During a scuffle at a fair, each, acting on his own, strikes Z once. Neither
saw the other strike. Can X be held liable under section 3(5) for the blow struck by Y?
- (A) Yes, because both intended to hurt Z.
- (B) Yes, because both were present at the scene when Z was struck.
- (C) No, because each held a similar intention separately; there was no meeting of minds, so the intention was not common.
- (D) No, because section 3(5) never applies to a fight that breaks out suddenly.

**3.** Seven villagers form an unlawful assembly whose common object is to use criminal force to harvest a crop from a disputed field. While they are at
work, one of them, P, takes out a pistol none of the others knew he had and shoots a passer-by with whom he has a private quarrel. Applying the passage,
are the other six guilty of the shooting under section 190?
- (A) Yes, because membership of an unlawful assembly at the time of the offence is always enough.
- (B) Yes, because the shooting happened while they were pursuing the common object.
- (C) No, because section 190 requires proof that each member personally did some act.
- (D) No, because the shooting was neither part of the common object nor known to be a likely result of pursuing it.

**4.** According to the passage, which of the following is **NOT** needed to make a member liable under section 190?
- (A) The assembly had five or more persons.
- (B) He was a member of the assembly when the offence was committed.
- (C) Proof that he personally did some act towards the offence.
- (D) The assembly's common object was one of those the statute lists.

**5.** Four men decide, in the moment, to rob a shopkeeper who is counting cash. One of them stabs the shopkeeper while the others grab the cash. The
prosecution charges all four with the stabbing only under section 190. Applying the passage, which is correct?
- (A) Section 190 cannot apply because four persons cannot form an unlawful assembly; liability, if any, must rest on section 3(5), under which a plan formed on the spot can suffice.
- (B) Section 190 applies because the men had a common object of robbery.
- (C) Neither section can apply, because the plan was not formed in advance.
- (D) Section 190 applies, but only to the man who stabbed the shopkeeper.
