## Lesson — "Which Article?": the route, not the subject

**Why this.** Your two GK-POL misses (Day 7) were both the same error: the headline was about *Governors and Bills*, so you
reached for Art. 200, but the question asked *how the matter reached the Court*. Polity questions built on current affairs
almost always ask for the **route**.

**The rule.** For any constitutional headline ask three things — **who is acting, towards whom, for what?** — and map:

| Who → whom | For what | Article |
|---|---|---|
| A person → Supreme Court | enforce a **fundamental right** (writs) | **32** (itself a fundamental right) |
| A person → High Court | fundamental rights **"and for any other purpose"** | **226** (wider than 32) |
| Union ↔ State, or State ↔ State → SC | a dispute about a **legal right** between governments | **131** (original, exclusive) |
| Anyone → SC, from any court/tribunal | permission to appeal | **136** (special leave) |
| President → SC | **advice** on a question of law or fact of public importance | **143** (heard by ≥ 5 judges, **Art. 145(3)**; the Court may decline, as it did on the Ayodhya reference in 1994) |
| State Bill → Governor | assent / withhold / return (not a Money Bill) / reserve for President | **200** |
| Reserved Bill → President | assent / withhold / direct Governor to return it | **201** |
| President → a State | President's Rule on failure of constitutional machinery | **356** (both Houses must approve within **two months**; S. R. Bommai, 1994 — subject to judicial review) |

**Worked example (AILET style).** "In 2025 the President asked the Supreme Court whether courts may fix timelines for
Governors to act on State Bills. The reference was made under: (A) Art. 200 (B) Art. 131 (C) Art. 143 (D) Art. 32."
Who → whom: *President → Supreme Court*; for what: *advice*. → **(C) Art. 143.** Art. 200 is what the question was
*about*; it is not how it got to the Court.

**The trap.** Examiners put the **subject** Article (200, 324, 21) next to the **route** Article (143, 32, 131). Two more
twins they love: 32 vs 226 (a High Court can issue writs for non-fundamental rights too; the Supreme Court under Art. 32
cannot), and 131 vs 32 (a *State government* suing the Union uses 131 — a State is not a citizen enforcing a fundamental
right).

**Passage (Questions 1–3) — 413 words**

A Governor in India occupies an office that is easy to misdescribe. Appointed by the President and holding office during the President's pleasure, the Governor can look like the Union's agent in the State capital. Yet the Constitution asks the same person to act, in nearly every matter, on the aid and advice of a Council of Ministers answerable to the State's elected Assembly. The office is a hinge, not a lock: it is meant to let two levels of government turn together, not to stop either from moving.

Nowhere is this tension sharper than in the passage of laws. When a Bill passed by the State legislature reaches the Governor, the Constitution offers a short menu: give assent, withhold it, return the Bill (unless it is a Money Bill) with a message asking the House to reconsider, or reserve it for the President. What the text does not offer is a fifth option — to hold the Bill indefinitely and say nothing. Silence, however, is precisely what several States have complained of. A measure that carries the sanction of a majority of elected legislators can sit on a desk for months, neither approved nor rejected, while the problem it was meant to address grows.

Defenders of the office reply that it exists to temper haste. A legislature that commands a large majority may pass a law that collides with the Constitution or with a central statute, and a pause for reflection is a safeguard rather than an obstruction. There is something in this. A Bill returned with a clear message gives the House a chance to correct itself, and the public a chance to see why.

But a safeguard that operates in silence is hard to tell from a veto. The difference between reflection and obstruction lies less in the length of the pause than in whether reasons are given. A Governor who returns a Bill and explains the objection has engaged with the legislature; one who simply waits has substituted a personal judgment for the judgment of the House, without accepting responsibility for it.

The deeper point concerns trust. Federal systems survive not because every conflict is settled by a court, but because officials on each side behave in ways the other side can predict. Fixed deadlines may or may not be the right remedy; that is for Parliament and the courts to settle. What cannot be left unsettled is the expectation that a constitutional office will act, and will say why.
