## Lesson — words in context: the secondary-meaning trap

**Why this.** AILET's English section is 50 of 150 questions, and a good share are "meaning in context" items, synonyms,
idioms and one-word substitutions. On Day 7 you lost "modest" by picking *inexpensive*: a real meaning, but not the one the
sentence used.

**The rule.** Examiners choose words with two or more common senses and put the *other* sense among the options. The
dictionary meaning you know best is the bait.

**Method — cover, predict, match (≈ 20 s):**
1. **Cover** the word and read the whole sentence, plus the one before it.
2. **Predict** a plain word of your own that fits the gap ("limited", "stop", "hopeful").
3. **Match** your prediction to the options. Then check the passage's signals: contrast words (*but, not, rather*), a
   definition after a dash or colon, or an example that shows the sense.

**Worked example (AILET style).** "The minister's reply was *guarded*: he confirmed the talks but would not say when they
began." Guarded means — (A) protected by sentries (B) cautious, giving little away (C) hostile (D) imprisoned.
Cover → predict "careful / cagey" → **(B)**. The colon gives you the definition: confirming one fact but holding back the
other is caution. (A) and (D) are the literal senses of *guard*.

**The trap.** In a legal passage, legal senses are bait: *arrest* the development of something means *halt it*, not take
it into custody; a *qualified* approval is a limited one, not one that has passed an exam; *discharge* a duty means
*perform* it. Ask: "Would a plain word like *stop / limited / perform* make the sentence work?"

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 382 words**

The best judgments of the last century share a quality that is easy to admire and hard to imitate: economy. A judge who
uses ten words where forty would do is not being curt; she is respecting a reader who must act on what she says — the
litigant, the trial court, the official who will enforce the order. Economy in this sense is not stinginess. It is the
discipline of saying exactly as much as the case requires, and no more.

The temptation runs the other way. A long opinion looks thorough, and thoroughness looks like care. So judgments swell
with recitals of every argument advanced, every precedent cited at the bar, every statute that might conceivably apply.
The reader who wades through eighty pages to find the two paragraphs that decide the appeal may be forgiven for thinking
that length has been mistaken for labour. Worse, the excess can arrest the development of the law: when a holding is
buried under passing remarks, later courts cannot tell what was decided, and lawyers mine the surplus for whatever suits
their client.

None of this means that a good judgment should be terse to the point of obscurity. A ruling that announces a result
without reasons fails a different duty — the duty to show the losing party why they lost. The aim is a qualified brevity:
short where the law is settled, patient where it is not. Some of the most respected judges were famously modest in their
claims, deciding no more than the dispute before them and leaving larger questions for a case that squarely raised them.
Their restraint was not timidity. It reflected a sober view of what one bench, hearing one set of facts, can wisely settle
for a whole country.

Reformers are sanguine that word limits, structured headings and a summary at the top of each judgment will cure the
problem. Such devices help, but they treat a habit of mind as if it were a formatting error. The deeper remedy lies in how
lawyers are trained: to ask, before writing a sentence, whom it serves. A judgment discharges its function when the
parties understand the outcome, the lower courts can apply the rule, and the public can see that the power to decide was
exercised with reason. Everything else is ornament.
