# Day 11 — Reading comprehension the CLAT way: map first, then hunt (CLAT English)

## Lesson

**Why this, today.** English is roughly a fifth of CLAT — four or five passages of about 450 words, each with five or
six questions. It is the section where graduates most often lose easy marks, not because the passages are hard but
because they read for pleasure and answer from memory. You have no data in it yet; today starts that.

### The method — "Map, then Hunt"
1. **Map (≈ 2½ minutes).** Read once at a steady pace. After each paragraph, give it a 3–5 word label in your head:
   *P1 setup — convenience praised; P2 cost — riders*. Note two things only: the **author's own view** (look for
   *but, however, the question is, none of this*) and **whose view is being reported** (*defenders say, firms insist*).
2. **Hunt (≈ 40 s per question).** For each question, go back to the paragraph your map points to and answer from
   the text — never from memory or from what you believe about the world.
3. **Eliminate by the four traps** (below). On CLAT, getting from four options to two is usually fast; the mark is
   won or lost between the last two.

### The question types you'll see
- **Main idea / central claim** — must cover the whole passage, including the author's turn. An option that states
  only one paragraph is too narrow; one that goes further than the author is too broad.
- **Inference** — what *must* be true from the text, one short step beyond it. Not what *might* be true.
- **Assumption** — the unstated link an argument needs. Test: if it were false, would the argument collapse?
- **Tone / attitude** — read the verbs and qualifiers. Most CLAT authors are *measured*: they concede, then disagree.
  Options like "scornful" or "wholehearted" are rarely right.
- **Strengthen / weaken** — attack or support the specific link in the paragraph asked about, not the topic in general.

### The four traps (cut these on sight)
1. **Extreme** — *always, never, only, ban, all*. Authors who write "none of this calls for a ban" don't want bans.
2. **Out of scope** — true in the world, absent from the passage.
3. **Wrong voice** — a view the author *reports* (defenders, firms) offered as the author's own.
4. **True but not the answer** — a correct detail from the passage that doesn't answer *this* question.

### Worked example (60 s)
*Paragraph:* "Supporters of open-plan offices say they spark collaboration. Some firms did report more conversation
in the first months. But conversation is not collaboration; much of it was small talk, and focused work moved to
headphones and evenings."
**Q. The author's attitude to the supporters' claim is best described as:** (A) complete rejection — open offices
produce no conversation; (B) acceptance — open offices aid collaboration; (C) partial concession — more talk
happened, but it doesn't amount to the collaboration claimed; (D) indifference.
**Answer: (C).** (A) is extreme and contradicted ("did report more conversation"); (B) is the wrong voice; (D) ignores
the "But". The "concede, then turn" shape is the single most common author stance in CLAT passages.

**The trap examiners set.** Two options that both sound like the author. One matches the text; the other adds a
step the author never took — a recommendation, a cause, a "therefore". When stuck between two, pick the one that
claims *less*.

## Test — 8 questions, CLAT pattern (+1 / −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted). Aim ≤ 8 minutes including reading.

**Passage (Q1–6)**

Ten years ago, a city resident who ran out of milk at night walked to the corner shop or did without. Today a phone
delivers it in minutes, and we have stopped noticing how strange that is. Quick-commerce companies describe this as
liberation from errands. That description is accurate as far as it goes; the question is how far it goes.

The first cost is borne by those who make the speed possible. A promise of ten minutes is not a promise about the
distance between a warehouse and a door; it is a promise about how a rider will behave on the road. Firms insist
that the clock starts only after packing and that no rider is penalised for lateness. Even if that is true, an
incentive need not be written down to be felt. A rider paid per delivery knows that the faster he returns, the more
he earns, and the countdown on the customer's screen tells him what is expected.

The second cost falls on the neighbourhood. The corner shop did more than sell milk. It extended credit to a family
between salaries, kept a spare key for an elderly neighbour, and noticed when a regular customer had not come by for
a week. None of this appears on a balance sheet, which is precisely why a business model that competes on price and
speed alone can destroy it without anyone deciding to.

Defenders of the model reply that consumers are adults who have voted with their phones, and that the shopkeeper's
charm was never a reason to pay more for worse service. There is force in this. Nostalgia is not an argument, and many
small shops did overcharge customers who had nowhere else to go. Quick commerce has also created work for many people
who had few other options.

But the defence assumes that what consumers choose in each transaction is what they would choose for their street as
a whole. These can differ. Each of us may prefer a ten-minute delivery tonight while preferring, on reflection, to live
somewhere a shopkeeper knows our name. Markets are good at adding up the first kind of preference and poor at
registering the second.

None of this calls for a ban. It calls for honesty about the trade. Cities could require that delivery-time promises
be removed from customer screens, so that speed is no longer a public countdown; labour rules could ensure that riders
are paid for time spent waiting; and residents could decide, now and then, that the walk to the corner is worth taking.
Convenience is a real good. It is simply not the only one.

**1.** Which of the following best expresses the central claim of the passage?
- (A) Quick commerce should be banned because it endangers riders and destroys small shops.
- (B) Quick commerce offers real benefits, but its costs to riders and neighbourhoods are obscured by individual choices and deserve to be acknowledged and managed.
- (C) Consumers who use quick-commerce apps are acting irrationally.
- (D) Corner shops provided better service than quick-commerce firms do.

**2.** When the author says that "an incentive need not be written down to be felt", the author most likely implies that:
- (A) quick-commerce firms are concealing penalties they impose on late riders.
- (B) a ten-minute delivery promise is physically impossible to meet.
- (C) riders should be paid a fixed monthly salary rather than per delivery.
- (D) riders may feel pressed to ride fast because of how they are paid and the visible countdown, whatever the firms' formal policy.

**3.** According to the author, the defenders' argument in the fourth paragraph rests on which assumption?
- (A) That the total of people's individual purchases reflects what they would want for their neighbourhood as a whole.
- (B) That small shops always overcharged their customers.
- (C) That riders prefer delivery work to every other kind of job.
- (D) That nostalgia is a valid reason to regulate markets.

**4.** The author's attitude towards the defenders of quick commerce is best described as:
- (A) scornful — their argument is dismissed as self-serving.
- (B) wholehearted agreement — consumer choice settles the matter.
- (C) neutral — the author reports both sides without taking a position.
- (D) a partial concession — the author grants some of their points but rejects their conclusion.

**5.** Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the author's argument in the third paragraph?
- (A) Studies find that in neighbourhoods where quick commerce has expanded, the number of small shops and the informal help they give residents have stayed the same.
- (B) Riders on quick-commerce platforms earn more than they did in their previous jobs.
- (C) Many corner shops charged more than the market price.
- (D) Most consumers say they value speed of delivery over price.

**6.** The author would most likely agree with which of the following?
- (A) Consumers who use quick commerce act against their own interests every time they order.
- (B) Banning quick commerce is the only way to protect neighbourhood life.
- (C) Some valuable features of a neighbourhood can disappear without anyone choosing to end them.
- (D) Quick commerce has created no worthwhile employment.

**7.** *(Review — Contract)* *Principle:* The communication of an acceptance is complete as against the proposer when it
is put in the course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the acceptor's power; and as against the acceptor when
it comes to the proposer's knowledge. The communication of a revocation is complete as against the person who makes it
when it is put in the course of transmission, and as against the person to whom it is made when it comes to his
knowledge. A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the
proposer, but not afterwards.
*Facts:* On 5 November Asha posts a letter offering to sell her car to Bilal for ₹4 lakh. It reaches him on 8 November.
On 10 November at 9 a.m. Asha posts a letter revoking the offer. At 11 a.m. the same day, Bilal posts his acceptance.
Asha's revocation reaches Bilal on 12 November; Bilal's acceptance reaches Asha on 13 November.
- (A) There is no contract, because Asha posted her revocation before Bilal posted his acceptance.
- (B) There is no contract, because the revocation reached Bilal before the acceptance reached Asha.
- (C) There is a contract, because Bilal's acceptance was complete as against Asha when he posted it on 10 November, before the revocation came to his knowledge.
- (D) There is a contract, because Bilal may choose whichever letter he prefers to rely on.

**8.** *(Review — Principle application)* *Principle:* Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, by reason of
a mistake of fact and not by reason of a mistake of law, in good faith believes himself to be justified by law in doing
it. Nothing is said to be done in good faith which is done without due care and attention.
*Facts:* Meera, a pharmacist, receives a carton invoiced and labelled as Drug X, which may lawfully be sold over the
counter. Before selling, she checks the batch code on the strips against the supplier's list of Drug X batches; it
matches. In fact the supplier had wrongly packed Drug Y, whose sale without prescription is prohibited. Meera sells a
strip and is prosecuted for selling Drug Y without a prescription.
- (A) Meera has no defence, because ignorance of the law is no excuse.
- (B) Meera has a defence, because her mistake was about what the product was — a fact — and she took due care by checking the batch code.
- (C) Meera has a defence, because a pharmacist cannot be expected to know every drug schedule.
- (D) Meera has no defence, because the drug she sold was in fact prohibited.
