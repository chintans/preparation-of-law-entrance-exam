## Lesson — CLAT current-affairs passages: the passage is the doorway, the hook is the room (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** Current Affairs is about 25% of CLAT, and you are at 0/2 on LEG-NEW and 0/3 on GK-POL. CLAT's CA questions start from a
news passage, but roughly half of them ask something the passage only *names*: the Article next to the one discussed, the new code
number, the body's head. The other day's method (FIHRR: Facts–Issue–Holding–Reason–Result) gets you the passage questions. Today
adds the second half: **expand every hook into its neighbours.**

**The method — Hook → Neighbours.** When a passage names a provision, spend 5 seconds recalling the three things around it:

| Hook in the passage | Neighbours CLAT asks about |
|---|---|
| **Art. 22(2)** — production before a Magistrate in 24 h | **22(1)** right to know the grounds of arrest and to consult a lawyer of one's choice · **22(3)** safeguards do not apply to an **enemy alien** or a person held under a **preventive-detention** law · **22(4)** preventive detention beyond **3 months** needs an **Advisory Board** opinion |
| **Art. 21** | Art. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended even in an Emergency (44th Amendment, 1978) · *D. K. Basu* (1997) arrest guidelines |
| **CrPC 57** (24-hour limit in the statute) | **BNSS 58** · remand **CrPC 167 → BNSS 187** · quashing **CrPC 482 → BNSS 528** |
| **NDPS s. 37** | "twin conditions" for bail in commercial-quantity cases; the Act dates from **1985** |

**Rule of thumb.** If a question's options are all Articles or all section numbers, the passage has stopped helping you. Answer
from the neighbours table, and eliminate the option that is *the Article the passage discussed*. Examiners plant it as the
"familiar" wrong answer. That is exactly your Art 143/200 miss.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *After a passage on the 24-hour rule:* "Under the Constitution, a person detained under a law
providing for preventive detention may not be held for longer than three months unless —" (A) a Magistrate authorises it under Art.
22(2) (B) an Advisory Board reports sufficient cause (C) the High Court issues a writ (D) the President consents.
The passage was about **22(2)**, which makes (A) the planted familiar option. But 22(3) takes preventive detention *out* of 22(2), and
22(4) supplies the rule → **(B)**.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **The familiar Article.** The provision the passage discussed is offered for a question about its neighbour.
2. **"Excluding journey time" only.** The 24 hours exclude travel to the Magistrate. They do **not** exclude nights, Sundays or holidays.
3. **Relief ≠ acquittal.** Bail for an Art. 22 breach leaves the trial running, just as quashing an FIR is not a finding of innocence.
4. **Old/new numbers swapped.** Learn them in pairs, old → new: 57→58, 167→187, 482→528, 498A→85, 304B→80.

**Passage (Questions 1–6) — 443 words**

Customs officers at Agartala stopped three men at about two in the afternoon of 29 September 2025 and found methamphetamine tablets, sold in the north-east as "yaba", in a quantity large enough to count as commercial under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act. The search ended around five. But the official record did not show the men as arrested until the next morning, a little after eleven, and they were brought before a Magistrate only at 4:45 that afternoon. Counted from the formal arrest, the officers had kept well inside the twenty-four hours the Constitution allows. Counted from the moment the men were stopped and held, they had not.

The Special Judge and then the Tripura High Court refused bail, pointing to Section 37 of the NDPS Act, which makes bail in commercial-quantity cases exceptionally hard to obtain. The point about the clock was raised for the first time in the Supreme Court.

Article 22(2) says that every person who is "arrested and detained in custody" must be produced before the nearest Magistrate within twenty-four hours, excluding the time needed for the journey, and cannot be held longer without the Magistrate's authority. In a judgment reported on 5 November 2026, Justices Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar read the two words together. For the purposes of the Article, the Court said, arrest and detention mean the same thing: the clock starts when a person's liberty is in fact curtailed, not when an officer later writes up an arrest memo. A "buffer zone" between the two would let investigators hold a person for as long as they pleased before the safeguard began, and is unconstitutional.

On that reading, the men stood arrested from 2 pm on 29 September, and their production more than a day later breached Article 22(2). The unexplained gap of some eighteen hours also offended Section 58 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, formerly Section 57 of the Code of Criminal Procedure. The Court then met the State's strongest argument. The rigour of Section 37, it held, must yield where Article 21 itself has been violated; a statute cannot be used to keep in custody a person whose custody began unlawfully. It allowed the appeals, set aside the High Court's order and directed that the men be released on bail.

The judgment also speaks to Magistrates. They must record the actual time a person was first detained, release anyone produced after twenty-four hours, and may refuse remand even within that period where the detention was unreasonably long. The men have not been acquitted; their trial will go on. What they won was a ruling on when the Constitution starts counting.
