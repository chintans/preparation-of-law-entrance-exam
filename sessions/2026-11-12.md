## Constitution Capsule — Unit 4: Preamble (≈ 4 min, before the clock)

**Five flash facts**
1. **The words** → "We, the people of India … **Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic**"; justice (social, economic, political),
   liberty (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), equality (status, opportunity), fraternity (dignity of the individual, **unity and
   integrity** of the Nation). Authority comes from **the people**.
2. **42nd Amendment, 1976** → added **"Socialist"**, **"Secular"** and **"and integrity"** — the **only** amendment to the Preamble so far.
   "Unity" was there from 1950; only "and integrity" was added. In *Dr Balram Singh v. UoI* (25 Nov 2024) the SC dismissed challenges to the 1976 additions.
3. **Objectives Resolution** → moved by **Nehru on 13 Dec 1946**, adopted 22 Jan 1947; the Preamble is built on it.
4. **Dates** → adopted and enacted "this **twenty-sixth day of November, 1949**" (Constitution Day); most of the Constitution came into force
   **26 Jan 1950**.
5. **Is it part of the Constitution?** → *Berubari Union* (1960): **no**, only a key to the framers' minds → *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973):
   **yes**, it can be amended under Art 368 but not so as to damage the basic structure (reaffirmed in *LIC v. Consumer Education* 1995). It is **not enforceable** in court on its own.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the facts and say: **42nd · 13 Dec 1946 · 26 Nov 1949 · Berubari 1960 · Kesavananda 1973.** Then check.

**Case.** *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973, **13 judges, 7:6**): Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, Preamble
included, but not its **basic structure**; the Preamble helps identify that structure.

**The trap.** Two of them: (1) *Berubari* said "not part" — examiners offer it as the current law; it was **overruled on this point by
Kesavananda**. (2) "Unity and integrity" — only **"integrity"** was added in 1976.

### English Language — Q1–4

**Passage (Q1–4) — 381 words.** *(Optional: time your read and send `R m:ss`. Level 1 target is 2:07 at 180 wpm.)*

When a phone's battery fails, the cheapest repair is often a new phone. That is not an accident of engineering. Over two decades, many manufacturers have glued batteries in place, used screws that ordinary tools cannot turn, and paired spare parts to a device's serial number so that a replacement screen, even a genuine one, triggers warnings unless the company itself installs it. Each choice can be defended on its own: glue makes a phone thinner, special screws deter tampering, pairing frustrates thieves who strip stolen phones for parts. Taken together, however, they move control over a product from the person who bought it to the firm that sold it.

The "right to repair" movement asks for that control back. Its demands are modest. Manuals, spare parts and diagnostic software should be available to owners and independent shops on fair terms, for a reasonable number of years after a model is sold. Several jurisdictions have adopted versions of these demands, and manufacturers that once warned of safety risks now advertise self-repair kits.

Critics make two serious points. First, repair done badly can be dangerous; a punctured lithium battery is a fire, not an inconvenience. Second, mandated stocks of spare parts cost money, and that cost is passed on to every buyer, including those who never repair anything. Both points deserve an answer, but neither is decisive. Electricians and car mechanics also work on dangerous systems, and society has managed that risk through training and liability rather than by forbidding anyone but the manufacturer to touch a wire. As for cost, a product that lasts eight years instead of four is cheaper per year of use even if it costs a little more on the day it is bought.

The deeper argument is about waste. Electronic waste is among the fastest-growing streams of rubbish in the world, and little of it is properly recycled. A phone that is repaired rather than replaced does not need to be mined, manufactured and shipped again. Recycling helps, but it recovers materials at the end of a product's life; repair extends the life itself.

None of this means every device must be built like a tractor. It means that the decision to end a product's life should rest, wherever possible, with the person who owns it.

### Legal Reasoning — Q10–14

**Passage (Q10–12) — 136 words**

Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of the right of private defence. Every person
has a right to defend his own body, and the body of any other person, against an offence affecting the human body. The right does not
arise where there is time to seek the protection of the public authorities. It does not extend to inflicting more harm than is necessary
for defence. The right to defend the body extends to causing death if the assault reasonably causes the apprehension that death or grievous
hurt will otherwise be the consequence. The right begins as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt
or threat to commit the offence, and it continues as long as that apprehension continues.

**10.** Late at night on a deserted road, B lunges at A with a long knife, shouting that he will kill him. A, who holds a licensed revolver,
fires once and B dies. Is A protected?
- (A) Yes — the assault reasonably caused an apprehension of death, so the right extended to causing death.
- (B) No — A should have tried to run away first.
- (C) No — the right of private defence never extends to causing death.
- (D) No — A should have called the police before acting.

**11.** A knocks the knife out of B's hand. B turns and runs. A chases him for two hundred metres, catches him and stabs him with the same
knife. Is A protected?
- (A) Yes — B had started the attack, so A could pursue him.
- (B) Yes — A was still angry and afraid.
- (C) No — the apprehension ended when B was disarmed and fled, and with it the right.
- (D) No — A used B's own knife.

**12.** On Monday B telephones A and says he will kill A when he comes to town on Friday. On Tuesday A waits for B outside B's house and
beats him badly. Is A protected?
- (A) Yes — a threat to kill always justifies force.
- (B) No — there was ample time to seek the protection of the public authorities, and no immediate danger.
- (C) Yes — the right begins as soon as a threat is made, whatever its timing.
- (D) No — threats made over the telephone are not offences.

**Passage (Q13–14) — 63 words**

A person who voluntarily agrees to run a risk, knowing its nature, cannot recover damages when harm results from that risk (*volenti non fit
injuria*). The consent covers only the risks ordinarily inherent in the activity; it does not cover harm caused by the defendant's negligence
beyond those risks. Mere knowledge of a danger is not the same as agreeing to bear it.

**13.** P buys a ticket for a cricket match and sits in the open stand. A batsman hits a six; the ball strikes P. P sues the club. Decide.
- (A) P succeeds — the club owed spectators complete safety.
- (B) P succeeds — P never signed a written waiver.
- (C) P fails — a ball entering the stand is a risk inherent in watching cricket, which P accepted by attending.
- (D) P fails — spectators can never sue a sports club.

**14.** At the same match, a wooden stand that the club had not inspected for years collapses, injuring Q. Decide.
- (A) Q succeeds — the collapse came from the club's negligence, a risk Q never agreed to bear.
- (B) Q fails — Q knew the stand was old and chose to sit there.
- (C) Q fails — Q accepted all risks by buying a ticket.
- (D) Q succeeds — only because the club makes a profit from the match.
