## Lesson — Natural justice in a legal passage: the rule against bias (≈ 6 min)

**The concept.** Two rules of natural justice run through every CLAT passage on a tribunal, inquiry or disciplinary action:
1. ***Nemo judex in causa sua*** — no one should be a judge in his own cause (**rule against bias**).
2. ***Audi alteram partem*** — hear the other side (**fair hearing**).
They are separate. Breaking either vitiates the decision, and **meeting one does not cure the other**: a perfect hearing before a biased judge is still void.

**The real case (17 Nov 2026).** In ***Ku. Kiran v. Sudhir Shikshan Sanstha*** (**Manoj Misra and Manmohan JJ**) a Nagpur school teacher was dismissed after a
three-member inquiry whose convenor was the **real brother of the management's Secretary** — the man who was the **sole witness on 11 of the 20 charges**.
The Supreme Court restored her **reinstatement with full back wages**. Its reasoning is the textbook test:
- The question is not whether the judge **was** biased but whether a fair-minded observer would see a **reasonable likelihood of bias**. **Actual bias need not be proved.**
- **Close kinship** is enough when it is close enough to raise that likelihood.
- Bias is a **foundational illegality**: cross-examination and a full hearing **cannot cure** it.
- **Doctrine of necessity** (a biased person may decide only if no one else can) did not apply — someone else could have been appointed.
- **Waiver** counts only if it is **voluntary, informed and unequivocal**.
The line of authority: ***A. K. Kraipak v. Union of India* (1969)** brought natural justice into administrative inquiries — there, a candidate for selection sat on
the selection board.

**Method — the bias checklist** (use it on any principle–fact question):
1. **Who decides?** Find the decision-maker, not the complainant.
2. **What is the link?** Money, family, prior involvement, being a witness. Ask: would a fair-minded outsider worry?
3. **Any escape?** Necessity (no one else can decide) or genuine waiver (knew everything, chose freely).
4. **Ignore the hearing.** How fair the procedure was is irrelevant to the bias question.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *Principle: a decision is void if the decision-maker has an interest giving rise to a reasonable likelihood of bias, even if no
actual bias is shown. Facts: a university's discipline committee of five expels a student for plagiarism. One member is the professor whose paper was copied.
He declares this but stays on and votes; the vote is 5–0. The student had a full hearing.*
→ **Void.** The professor is effectively the complainant judging his own cause. The 5–0 vote doesn't help — his participation is the defect, not his vote
being decisive. The full hearing doesn't help — that is *audi alteram partem*, a different rule.

**The traps.** (1) **"No evidence that he was actually biased"** — irrelevant; the test is *likelihood*. (2) **"The vote was unanimous, so his vote made no
difference"** — the presence of a biased member taints the body. (3) **"She was given every opportunity to cross-examine"** — answers the wrong rule.
(4) **Necessity** — only when there is *no one else*; it is never a matter of convenience.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 7 again: Article 19 (≈ 4 min)

*Yesterday's capsule went unread, so it comes round once more — this time as questions. Cover each answer and say it before you read it.*

**Five flash facts**
1. **Who gets Art 19?** → **Citizens only** (Arts 15, 16, 19, 29, 30 are the citizens-only FRs; Art 14 and Art 21 cover **any person**).
2. **How many freedoms now, and which one was dropped?** → **Six**: 19(1)(a) speech, (b) assembly *peaceably and without arms*, (c) associations (co-operatives
   added by the **97th Am. 2011**), (d) movement, (e) residence, (g) profession/trade → **(f) property deleted by the 44th Am. (1978)**.
3. **On what grounds can speech be restricted?** → **Eight, in Art 19(2)**: sovereignty & integrity (**16th Am. 1963**), security of the State, friendly relations
   with foreign States, **public order (1st Am. 1951)**, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence. Note what is *not* there:
   "**interests of the general public**" — that phrase belongs to 19(5) and 19(6), for movement, residence and trade.
4. **Where is press freedom?** → Not named; it is **inside 19(1)(a)** — ***Romesh Thappar* (1950)**; **internet speech and trade** protected and shutdowns must be
   proportionate — ***Anuradha Bhasin* (2020)**.
5. **What happens to Art 19 in an emergency?** → **Art 358** suspends it **automatically**, but **only** for an emergency declared on **war or external aggression**
   — **not** for armed rebellion (44th Am.). Contrast **Art 359**: other FRs need a Presidential order, and **Arts 20 and 21 can never be suspended**.

**Say-it drill.** **19(1)(a)–(g) · 19(2) · Romesh Thappar · Shreya Singhal · Art 358.** Say: the count of freedoms (6), of grounds (8), the deleted clause
(f, 44th Am.), and the one emergency ground on which Art 358 does *not* apply (armed rebellion).

**Case.** ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015)** — **s. 66A, IT Act struck down**: "grossly offensive" and "annoyance" were vague and fell outside the
eight grounds; only **incitement** (not discussion or advocacy) may be punished.

**The trap.** A restriction on speech "in the **interests of the general public**" or "to protect **public interest**" sounds valid — it is **not one of the
19(2) grounds**. Examiners slip it into a list of real ones.

**Passage 1 (Questions 1–5) — 395 words**

Natural justice rests on two short rules. The first is that no one may be a judge in his own cause; the second, that no one may be condemned unheard. Courts in India have long treated the first rule as the stricter of the two, because a hearing can be repaired by holding it again, while a tainted judge taints everything he touches.

The Supreme Court restated the first rule in November 2026 in the case of a teacher at a privately managed, government-aided school in Nagpur. After she complained that her name had been left off the salary-grant lists, the management charged her with twenty acts of misconduct. A three-member inquiry committee found her guilty by a majority and she was dismissed. The committee's convenor was the real brother of the management's Secretary, and the Secretary was the only witness on eleven of the charges.

The management argued that the teacher had been given every document, had cross-examined every witness and had not objected to the committee when it was formed. None of this, the Court held, could save the inquiry. The test for bias is not whether the inquiring officer was in fact partial, which can rarely be proved, but whether a fair-minded observer, knowing the facts, would think there was a real likelihood that he might be. Close kinship with the person who brings the charge and proves it is enough. Bias, the Court said, is a defect at the foundation; a fair procedure built on top of it does not cure it.

Two exceptions were argued and rejected. The doctrine of necessity allows an interested person to decide when no one else is legally able to, so that the matter is not left undecided; here the management could easily have appointed someone else. Waiver allows a party to give up an objection, but only if she knew the facts and chose freely and clearly to accept the decision-maker; silence by an employee facing her own employer's committee is not such a choice.

The Court set aside the High Court's ruling and restored the School Tribunal's order reinstating the teacher with full back wages. The judgment follows a line that runs back to 1969, when the Court held that a selection board was vitiated because one of the candidates sat on it, even though he withdrew when his own name came up.

- (C) The decision is void if the inquiring officer has any relationship, however distant, with any party.
