## Lesson — Summits and groupings: four slots (≈ 6 min)

**Why it matters.** Mid-November to mid-December is summit season: ASEAN-India and the East Asia Summit (Manila, 17 Nov), APEC (Shenzhen, 18–19
Nov), the G20 (Miami, 14–15 Dec). CLAT's December paper is written months earlier, but its GK passages love a summit — and the questions are
almost never about what was *said*. They test four things you can know in advance.

**Method — four slots per grouping.**
1. **Edition + host** — the number and the city/chair. Twin summits on one day carry **different** numbers (23rd ASEAN-India, 21st EAS).
2. **Membership** — the count, and whether India is a **member, partner or outsider**.
3. **Anchor date** — founding year and the founding document or city.
4. **India's hook** — the policy, partnership level or agreement India ties to it.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *"As leaders of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum met in Shenzhen, India's absence was noted again."*
Slots: **Edition/host** 2026, China (Shenzhen) · **Membership** **21 economies; India is not a member** (it applied in the 1990s; a moratorium on new
members kept it out) · **Anchor** founded **1989**, Canberra; secretariat **Singapore** · **India's hook** none — which is the point.
Question: *"Which of the following is NOT a member of APEC? (a) Russia (b) Chinese Taipei (c) India (d) Peru"* → **(c)**. Mark each option T/F against
the Membership slot before choosing — it is a NOT-stem.

**The traps.** (1) **Chair ≠ seat**: the Philippines chairs ASEAN in 2026, but the ASEAN Secretariat is in **Jakarta**. (2) **Member vs partner**:
India is a *dialogue partner* of ASEAN and a *participant* in the EAS — never an ASEAN member. (3) **Look East (1991) vs Act East (2014)** — same
policy, two names, two dates; the 2014 renaming was announced at the **East Asia Summit in Naypyidaw**.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 6 again: Articles 14–18 (≈ 4 min)

*Repeated once because yesterday's set was missed. Same unit, new angle: say each fact as "Article → test → case".*

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 14** → **any person** (citizen or not) → classification passes only with **intelligible differentia + rational nexus** (*Anwar Ali Sarkar*, 1952);
   arbitrariness itself breaks Art 14 (***E. P. Royappa***, 1974).
2. **Art 15(4)** → special provisions for backward classes, SC/ST → added by the **1st Amendment (1951)** after ***Champakam Dorairajan***.
3. **Art 16(4)** → reservation in posts → ***Indra Sawhney*** (1992, **9 judges**): **50% ceiling**, **creamy layer** out, none in promotions → reversed for
   SC/ST by **16(4A)** (77th Am. 1995).
4. **Arts 15(6) + 16(6)** → **EWS up to 10%** → **103rd Amendment (2019)** → upheld 3:2 in ***Janhit Abhiyan*** (2022).
5. **Art 17** → untouchability abolished, enforced by the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** · **Art 18** → no titles (military and academic
   distinctions excepted); ***Balaji Raghavan*** (1996): **Bharat Ratna / Padma awards are not titles**, but cannot be used as a prefix or suffix.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the list: **14 · 15(4) · 16(4) / 16(4A) · 15(6) + 16(6) · 17 · 18.** For each, say the amendment *and* the case.

**Case.** ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India*** (1992, 9 judges): upheld 27% OBC reservation in central posts, fixed the **50% cap**, excluded the
**creamy layer**, and barred reservation in **promotions** — the line every later amendment reacts to.

**The trap.** Art 18 has **two exceptions** written into it — **military and academic** distinctions. A Padma award is not an exception: it is simply
*not a title at all*. Options saying "permitted under the exception in Art 18(1)" are the decoy.

**Passage 1 (Questions 1–3) — 387 words**

When Prime Minister Narendra Modi landed in Manila on 16 November 2026, he walked into the busiest diplomatic week of the Philippine year. As ASEAN Chair for 2026, the Philippines was hosting both the 23rd ASEAN-India Summit and the 21st East Asia Summit on 17 November. India and the Southeast Asian bloc have designated 2026 the ASEAN-India Year of [1], and in 2027 they will mark 35 years of dialogue relations and five years of their Comprehensive Strategic Partnership.

The relationship began modestly. India became a sectoral dialogue partner of ASEAN in 1992, a year after New Delhi announced its Look East Policy in the shadow of a balance-of-payments crisis. Summit-level meetings followed in 2002. In [2], the policy was renamed Act East, a change meant to signal that India would do more than watch the region: it would build roads, ports and security ties there. In his departure statement, Mr Modi called ASEAN the heart of that policy and central to India's MAHASAGAR vision for the Indian Ocean.

The East Asia Summit is a different creature. Established in 2005, it brings the ASEAN members together with eight partners: India, China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, the United States and Russia. It is a forum for leaders rather than an organisation with a secretariat of its own, and it works by consensus. That makes it useful for conversation but slow at producing binding commitments; disputes in the South China Sea have been discussed there for two decades without a settled code of conduct.

Trade remains the sore point. India's imports from ASEAN have grown faster than its exports, and New Delhi has long pressed for a review of the goods trade agreement that came into force in 2010. Indian negotiators argue that its rules of origin are too loose, letting goods made outside the bloc enter India at ASEAN tariff rates. Southeast Asian governments reply that India's own tariffs and standards rules make its market harder to enter than it looks.

Maritime cooperation is where interests overlap most clearly. Several ASEAN members contest Chinese claims at sea, and India has offered coastal surveillance, joint naval exercises and, in the Philippines' case, BrahMos missiles. Whether that cooperation can deepen without either side being drawn into the other's disputes is the question Manila was expected to leave open.

**Passage 2 (Questions 4–8) — 427 words**

Should India's Supreme Court be split in two? An opinion piece published on 14 November 2026 revived a question that has circulated for nearly four decades. Its argument begins with numbers. The Court now has a sanctioned strength of 38 judges, raised from 34 earlier this year, yet more than 90,000 cases are pending before it. Most are ordinary appeals — property disputes, bail matters, service claims — that have already been through at least two courts.

The framers had a narrower picture in mind. They expected the Court to settle the great questions of constitutional meaning through benches of five or more judges, as Article 145(3) requires. In the 1960s such benches delivered well over a hundred judgments a year; today the figure is often in single digits. The author's diagnosis is that a court trying to correct every error ends up with too little time to say what the law is.

The remedy proposed is structural. A Constitutional Court of eleven judges would sit in Delhi and hear only constitutional questions and matters of national importance. Four or five regional Courts of Appeal would hear the ordinary appeals, bringing the highest tier of justice closer to litigants from the south, the east and the north-east, who must now travel to Delhi and pay Delhi lawyers.

The idea is not new. In 2009 the Law Commission, in its [1] Report, recommended a Constitution Bench at Delhi and cassation benches in four regions. A parliamentary standing committee endorsed regional benches in 2023. In 2016 a three-judge bench, hearing V. Vasanthakumar v. H. C. Bhatia, referred the question of a National Court of Appeal to a Constitution Bench. That reference has never been heard.

Part of the plan may not need an amendment at all. Article [2] already allows the Court to sit in Delhi "or in such other place or places" as the Chief Justice of India may appoint with the approval of the President. Regional benches of the same Court could be created this way. A separate Court of Appeal whose decisions were final, however, would be a new court, and creating one would require Parliament to amend the Constitution.

The Court itself has been cool to the idea. Its judges worry that benches scattered across the country would drift apart, producing conflicting rulings in a system where the law declared by the Supreme Court binds every court in India. Supporters reply that a court which takes years to decide a bail appeal has already given up on uniformity of a more basic kind: equal access to justice.
