## Current-Affairs Brief (5 min, untimed)

1. **A police statement cannot beat sworn evidence (SC, *Naresh v. State of Haryana*, 16 Nov 2026)** — *LEG-NEW.* A bench of Satish Chandra Sharma and N. Kotiswar Singh JJ
   set aside a double-murder conviction. A witness had said one thing to the police and another in court, and the trial court had preferred the police version. The SC
   held that a court cannot do that. A proved contradiction can only **impeach the witness's credit**; it is not itself evidence of the facts. *Hook:* police record
   statements under **s. 161 CrPC = s. 180 BNSS**. Under **s. 162 CrPC = s. 181 BNSS**, such a statement is not signed and can be used only to **contradict** the witness.
   *Likely Q:* "A statement to the police during investigation can be used at trial to…" (contradict the witness, not as substantive evidence)
2. **Recovery of bribe money alone cannot prove corruption (SC, *Har Parsad Gaur v. State (NCT of Delhi)*, 17 Nov 2026)** — *LEG-NEW.* Ujjal Bhuyan and Atul S. Chandurkar JJ
   acquitted a former Delhi tehsildar. The complainant and the panch (shadow) witness never testified. The trap officer's account of what the panch told him was
   **hearsay**, so the **demand** for a bribe was never proved, and recovery of marked notes could not fill the gap. *Hook:* **Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988**:
   **s. 7** (public servant taking a bribe), **s. 13(1)(d)** (criminal misconduct, pre-2018 text), **s. 20** (presumption, which arises only after acceptance is proved).
   ***Neeraj Dutta*** (2022, 5 judges) held that **demand and acceptance** must both be proved, though circumstantial evidence can do it. *Likely Q:* "To convict under s. 7 PC
   Act, the prosecution must prove…" (demand and acceptance, not just recovery)
3. **West Bengal cabinet approves the name "Paschim Banga" (23 Nov 2026)** — *GK-POL.* Chief Minister **Suvendu Adhikari** said the proposal goes to the State Assembly on
   **9 Dec 2026**. Earlier attempts (Paschim Banga in 2011, "Bangla" in 2018) never got through the Centre. *Hook:* a State's name can be changed only by **Parliament

   **simple majority**, and **Art 4** says it is **not** an amendment under Art 368. *Likely Q:* "Which Article lets Parliament alter the name of a State?" (Art 3)

## Lesson — CLAT current-affairs passages: P or B? (≈ 6 min)

**How the section works.** CLAT gives you a 400–450-word news passage followed by five or six questions. Some are answered **in the passage (P)**: what it says,
implies or argues. Others go **beyond it (B)**: the Article, the body, the treaty, the place the passage names but doesn't explain. Most candidates read every
question as P and hunt the passage for an answer that isn't there. That wastes time, and it makes a planted, passage-sounding option look right.

**The method: label every stem P or B before you read the options.**
- **P-stems** say *"according to the passage"*, *"the author suggests"*, *"which is supported by"* or *"the passage implies"*. Answer **only** from the text, and test each
  option against one line of it.
- **B-stems** name a fact and ask what lies behind it: *"…is located in"*, *"…is governed by"*, *"…was established under"*, *"which of the following is NOT…"*. Don't
  look in the passage. Climb the hook ladder (law → body → number) and answer from what you know. If you don't know, strike what you can and decide whether to guess.
- **Time:** a B-question you know takes 15 seconds, and one you don't know shouldn't take more than 30. Save the minutes for the P-questions.

**Worked example.** The passage, from yesterday's brief: *"COP31 in Antalya closed on 22 November without the proposed 'Antalya Pact'. The EU and small island states
objected that the draft did not name coal, oil or gas…"*
- *"According to the passage, the talks ended without a deal mainly because…"* → **P.** The answer is the objection that the draft did not name fossil fuels, nothing more.
- *"The secretariat of the convention under which COPs are held is located in…"* → **B.** The passage never says. Hook: the UNFCCC (1992) has its secretariat in
  **Bonn**. Reject "New York (UN HQ)": it is true of the UN, but it is the wrong body.
- *"COP32 will be held in…"* → **B** (recall). The answer is **Addis Ababa**. Antalya is the trap, because it is in the passage.

**The traps examiners set**
- **The passage word in a B-question.** If a B-option simply repeats a name from the passage, be suspicious. It is often there to catch P-readers.
- **True, but not what was asked.** A correct fact about the wrong body (UN vs UNFCCC, RBI vs NPCI) is still wrong.
- **NOT-stems in B-questions.** Mark each option T or F against what you know. Then choose the F.

**CLAT marking reminder:** a blank is **0**. Guess on a B-question only if you can strike at least one option. That makes the average +0.17. A blind guess averages +0.06.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 10 again: Articles 23–30, second pass (≈ 4 min)

Yesterday's capsule went unanswered, so Arts 23–30 run once more. This time, learn **who holds each right**, and the fine print in Arts 27, 28 and 30.

**Five flash facts**
1. **Who holds it:** **Art 25** belongs to **all persons**, including foreigners. **Art 26** belongs to **every religious denomination**. **Art 29(1)** belongs to **any section
   of citizens** with a distinct language, script or culture. **Art 30(1)** belongs to **all minorities, religious or linguistic**.
2. **Art 25's two Explanations:** wearing and carrying a **kirpan** is part of the Sikh religion (Expl. I). In 25(2)(b), **"Hindus"** includes **Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists**
   (Expl. II), so throwing open Hindu religious institutions covers them too.
3. **Art 27** → no **tax** whose proceeds are spent on promoting a particular religion. A **fee** for services, such as a temple-administration fee, is allowed (*Shirur Mutt*, 1954).
4. **Art 28 has three tiers:**
   - an institution **wholly maintained by State funds** may give no religious instruction (28(1));
   - one run by the State under an endowment or trust that requires religious instruction may give it (28(2));
   - one that is State-recognised or State-aided may give it only with the student's **consent**, or a guardian's for a minor (28(3)).
5. **Art 30(1A)** (44th Am., 1978) → if the State acquires a minority institution's property, the compensation must not **restrict or abrogate** the Art 30 right.
   **Art 30(2)**: the State may not discriminate against a minority institution when giving **aid**.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the lines and say what each does: **25 · 26 · 27 · 28 · 30(1A)**.

**Case:** ***Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986).** Three Jehovah's Witness children who stood respectfully but did not sing the national anthem had been expelled. The SC
ordered them reinstated: no law required them to sing, and their silence was protected by **Arts 19(1)(a) and 25**.

**The trap:** "No religious instruction in **any** State-aided institution" is wrong. The bar in 28(1) applies only to institutions **wholly maintained** by the State, and aided
ones need consent. "Art 29 protects only minorities" is wrong too: it protects **any section of citizens**.

Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah chose New Delhi for his first bilateral visit since taking office, and the two days he spent there, 22 and 23 November 2026, ended with an Indian assistance package of ₹15,000 crore. The headline number hides an important distinction. Only ₹3,500 crore is a grant, money that never has to be repaid, earmarked largely for reconstruction and infrastructure. The remaining ₹11,500 crore is a line of credit in rupees, meant mainly for the energy sector, which Nepal must eventually repay with interest. Read carefully, then, the package is less a gift than an investment both sides expect to recover.

The projects show where the two governments see the future. The foundation stone was laid for the 669-megawatt Lower Arun hydroelectric project, and a bridge linking Dharchula in India with Darchula in Nepal across the Mahakali river was opened. A new train, the Sarayu-Kamala Express, will run between Ayodhya and Janakpur, two towns joined in popular memory by the Ramayana. For a landlocked country, the most practical gain may be access to more Indian ports for its trade: Deendayal Port at Kandla was opened to Nepal's transit cargo.

The relationship rests on an unusual foundation. A treaty signed in 1950 lets the citizens of each country live and work in the other, and the long border, which Nepal shares with five Indian States, is open. Openness has not meant agreement. In 2020 Kathmandu published a political map claiming Kalapani, Lipulekh and Limpiyadhura, territory that India regards as its own, and the issue returns whenever trade through the Lipulekh pass is discussed.

Neither side wanted this visit to be about disputes. The leaders adopted a Joint Vision titled "Viksit Bharat and Samunnat Nepal" and exchanged eight memoranda of understanding on subjects ranging from police training to a satellite network using the South Asia Satellite. Critics in Kathmandu argue that Nepal has signed many such documents before and that delivery, not vision, is what has been missing. Supporters answer that hydropower is different. Nepal can sell electricity to India, they say, and the money earned from those sales can repay the very credit that builds the plants. If they are right, the line of credit is not a burden but a bridge, and a sturdier one than the new span at Dharchula.

**Legal reasoning (Questions 6–8) — 58 words.** *Principle:* Whoever, by deceiving any person, fraudulently or dishonestly induces the person so deceived to deliver any
property, commits cheating. A mere failure to keep a promise is not cheating. The deception must exist at the time the promise is made, that is, the person must have
intended not to keep it from the start.

**6.** *Facts:* A borrows ₹50,000 from B and promises to repay it within a month. A means to repay, but his shop is flooded two weeks later and he cannot. B says A
cheated him. Applying the principle:
- (A) A has cheated B, because B delivered money and was not repaid.
- (B) A has cheated B, because A should have foreseen the flood.
- (C) A has not cheated B, because A intended to repay when he made the promise; his later failure is only a broken promise.
- (D) A has not cheated B, because a loan between friends can never be cheating.

**7.** *Facts:* A sells B a chain, saying it is 22-carat gold. A knows it is gold-plated brass. B pays ₹60,000. Applying the principle:
- (A) A has cheated B, because he deceived B about the chain and so induced B to hand over the money.
- (B) A has not cheated B, because B could have had the chain tested before paying.
- (C) A has not cheated B, because A delivered a chain as promised.
- (D) A has cheated B only if B suffered a loss of more than ₹50,000.

**8.** *Facts:* A, who owns no car, takes ₹2 lakh from B as an advance for "his" car. He promises to deliver it in a week, then switches off his phone and leaves town.

- (A) A has not cheated B, because a failure to deliver under a contract is only a civil wrong.
