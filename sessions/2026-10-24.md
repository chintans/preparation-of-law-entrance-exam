# Day 5 — Contract review: the two clocks, and the four ways a proposal dies (NLSAT Part A)

## Lesson

**Why again.** On Day 2 you missed two timing questions (Q5, Q7). Both came from mixing up *whose* clock
runs when. Today: fix that for good, then add s. 6 of the Indian Contract Act — how a proposal ends even
without a contract.

**1. The two clocks (ss. 4–5).** Ask one question: *"Complete as against whom?"*

| Event | Complete against the **sender** | Complete against the **receiver** |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance | — | Proposer is bound when it is **posted**; acceptor is bound when it **reaches** the proposer |
| Revocation (of offer or of acceptance) | when **posted** | when it **reaches** the other side |

Deadlines that follow:
- **Proposer can revoke** until the acceptance is **posted**. His revocation counts only if it **reaches**
  the acceptor before that.
- **Acceptor can revoke** her acceptance until it **reaches** the proposer. Her revocation counts only if it
  **reaches** the proposer first.
- *Rule of thumb:* a revocation always has to **arrive** in time. Its posting date almost never decides anything.

**2. How a proposal comes to an end (s. 6)** — four ways:
1. **Notice of revocation** by the proposer to the other party (timed as above).
2. **Lapse of time** — the time stated in the proposal, or, if none, a **reasonable time** (shorter for
   perishable goods or volatile prices; longer for land).
3. **Failure of a condition precedent** — if the proposal says "accept only after you pay X", an acceptance
   without paying X is no acceptance.
4. **Death or insanity of the proposer — but only if the acceptor comes to know of it before accepting.**
   This is where Indian law differs from the common English position: an acceptor who accepts *in
   ignorance* of the proposer's death can still conclude a contract (enforceable against the estate, for
   contracts that are not personal).

**Worked example (NLSAT style).**
*Principle:* a proposal lapses at the end of the time it fixes for acceptance.
*Facts:* On 1 July Farhan writes to Gita offering his tractor for ₹5 lakh, "open until 10 July". Gita posts
her acceptance on 12 July; it reaches Farhan on 14 July. Farhan never wrote to withdraw the offer.
*Working:* The deciding fact is the date of acceptance against the deadline — 12 July is after 10 July. The
proposal had already died by lapse; there was nothing to accept. **No contract.** The tempting option —
"Farhan never revoked his offer" — is the trap: lapse needs no revocation.

**The traps examiners set.**
- Comparing the wrong two dates (e.g. revocation *posted* vs acceptance *posted*). Always compare the
  revocation's **arrival** with the relevant deadline.
- "The proposer never withdrew, so the offer was open" — lapse and failed conditions end an offer silently.
- Applying the English death rule. Under the Act, the acceptor's **knowledge** is the hinge.
- In NLSAT Part A, an **unanswered** question also costs −0.25. Eliminate and commit.

## Test — 8 questions, aim for ~8 minutes, +1 / −0.25 (wrong *or* unanswered)

### Passage (Q1–8)

When two people make a bargain by letter, neither can see the other change his mind, and the law must
therefore fix a moment at which each communication takes effect. The person who makes a proposal is bound
from the moment the other party's acceptance is put in the course of transmission to him, so that the
acceptor can no longer recall it. The acceptor, by contrast, is bound only when her acceptance comes to the
knowledge of the proposer. It follows that the proposer may revoke his proposal up to the moment the
acceptance is sent, and the acceptor may revoke her acceptance up to the moment it reaches the proposer. In
each case a revocation takes effect against the other party only when it comes to that party's knowledge;
the date on which it was written or sent binds only its sender.

A proposal may also come to an end without any revocation. If it states a time within which it must be
accepted, it lapses when that time runs out. If it states no time, it lapses after a reasonable time, and
what is reasonable depends on the nature of the bargain: an offer of fresh produce or of goods whose price
changes daily must be accepted quickly, an offer of land may remain open longer. If the proposal requires
the acceptor to do something before accepting, an acceptance given without doing it is no acceptance at all.

Lastly, a proposal is revoked by the death or insanity of the proposer, but only if that fact comes to the
knowledge of the acceptor before she accepts. An acceptor who accepts in good faith, not knowing that the
proposer has died, is not to be prejudiced by a fact she could not have known.

*(Each question is independent.)*

**1.** On 2 March Hari posts a letter to Isha offering to sell his printing press for ₹6 lakh. On 4 March he
posts a letter revoking the offer; it reaches Isha on 6 March. Isha posts her acceptance on 7 March. Is there
a contract?
- (A) Yes, because Hari's proposal was accepted unconditionally.
- (B) No, because Hari's revocation came to Isha's knowledge on 6 March, before she sent her acceptance.
- (C) No, because Hari posted his revocation on 4 March, before Isha accepted.
- (D) Yes, because Isha's acceptance bound Hari the moment it was posted, whatever he had done earlier.

**2.** Hari's offer is the same. Isha posts her acceptance on 5 March; it reaches Hari on 8 March. On 6 March
Isha regrets her decision and sends Hari a courier letter revoking her acceptance, which reaches him on
9 March. Is there a contract?
- (A) No, because Isha revoked her acceptance before Hari had replied to it.
- (B) No, because Isha's revocation was sent on 6 March, before her acceptance reached Hari.
- (C) Yes, because Isha's acceptance reached Hari on 8 March, before her revocation reached him on 9 March.
- (D) Yes, because an acceptance, once posted, can never be revoked.

**3.** On 1 August Jaya offers by letter to sell 500 kg of fresh lychees to Kunal at ₹200 per kg. The letter
states no time for acceptance. Kunal posts his acceptance on 29 August, by which time the lychee season is
over. Jaya refuses to supply. Which is the best view under the passage?
- (A) A contract exists, because Jaya never revoked her offer.
- (B) A contract exists, because the offer stated no time limit and so stayed open until withdrawn.
- (C) No contract, because the offer had probably lapsed: a reasonable time for accepting an offer of fresh produce had passed.
- (D) No contract, because Jaya changed her mind after 1 August.

**4.** Leela offers to sell her flat to Mohan for ₹80 lakh, "acceptance to be valid only on payment of a
₹2 lakh token amount to my account". Mohan writes back, "I accept your offer; the token will follow next
week," and pays nothing. Leela then sells the flat to someone else. Was there a contract between Leela and
Mohan?
- (A) No, because Mohan's acceptance was given without first doing what the proposal required.
- (B) Yes, because Mohan accepted all of Leela's terms in writing.
- (C) Yes, because a promise to pay the token is as good as payment.
- (D) No, because Leela was entitled to prefer a higher offer from another buyer.

**5.** On 1 May Naveen posts an offer to sell his antique clock to Pooja. Naveen dies suddenly on 3 May. Pooja,
who lives in another city and has heard nothing of his death, posts her acceptance on 4 May. Under the
passage, is the proposal revoked?
- (A) Yes, because a proposal always ends with the death of the proposer.
- (B) No, because the fact of Naveen's death did not come to Pooja's knowledge before she accepted.
- (C) Yes, because a dead person cannot be bound by a contract.
- (D) No, because the proposal was posted before Naveen died.

**6.** In the facts of Q5, which one of the following additional facts, if true, would change the answer?
- (A) Naveen's son did not want the clock sold.
- (B) Pooja's acceptance reached Naveen's house only on 6 May.
- (C) On the morning of 4 May, before posting her acceptance, Pooja read a news report of Naveen's death.
- (D) Pooja learnt of Naveen's death on 7 May, after posting her acceptance.

**7.** Ravi posts an offer to Sunita. Sunita posts her acceptance on 10 June. Ravi sends a revocation of his
offer by speed post on 9 June; it reaches Sunita on 11 June. Ravi argues that his revocation was sent first
and so must win. Which statement best answers him?
- (A) Ravi is right: the earlier communication always prevails.
- (B) Ravi is wrong: his revocation had to come to Sunita's knowledge before she sent her acceptance, and it reached her only on 11 June.
- (C) Ravi is right: Sunita's acceptance reached him only after he had sent his revocation.
- (D) Ravi is wrong: an offer, once posted, can never be revoked.

**8.** Which one of the following can be **inferred** from the passage?
- (A) A proposer who wants to be sure of escaping his proposal must make his revocation reach the other party before she sends her acceptance.
- (B) An acceptor can never withdraw her acceptance once she has posted it.
- (C) Every proposal remains open until the proposer expressly withdraws it.
- (D) The death of an acceptor revokes a proposal.
