## Lesson — The FIR under BNSS s. 173 (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** LEG-NEW is your most overdue topic (0/2) and the heaviest CLAT weight you have not built. The new criminal laws are
the single most likely "legal current affairs" passage source, and CLAT tests them by giving you a rule and a fact pattern.

**The three Sanhitas — numbers to carry (in force 1 July 2024)**

| New law | Replaced | Sections | Hooks most asked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) | IPC 1860 (511 ss.) | 358 | murder s. 103 (mob lynching s. 103(2)); theft s. 303; organised crime s. 111; terrorist act s. 113; community service a new punishment (s. 4(f)); sedition dropped — s. 152 (acts endangering sovereignty) |
| Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) | CrPC 1973 (484 ss.) | 531 | FIR s. 173 (was s. 154); Magistrate's order to investigate s. 175(3) (was 156(3)); s. 218 sanction (was 197); trial in absentia s. 356 |
| Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) | Evidence Act 1872 (167 ss.) | 170 | electronic records are documents; s. 63 certificate (was 65B) |

**The FIR rule in four steps (s. 173 BNSS):**
1. **Where?** Any police station, *irrespective of the area* where the offence happened — the **zero FIR**, now in the text.
2. **How?** Orally or by **electronic communication**; an e-FIR is taken on record once the informant **signs within 3 days**.
3. **Must they register?** *Lalita Kumari* (Constitution Bench, 2013): yes, if a cognizable offence is disclosed; a preliminary
   inquiry only in narrow categories (matrimonial, commercial, medical negligence, corruption, delay). **s. 173(3)** adds a general
   exception: for offences punishable **≥ 3 years but < 7 years**, with prior permission of an officer **not below DSP**, the
   officer **may** hold a preliminary enquiry to see within **14 days** whether a prima facie case exists. *Imran Pratapgadhi v.
   State of Gujarat* (SC, 28 Mar 2025; Oka, Bhuyan JJ): for speech-related offences in that band, police should ordinarily use it,
   because Art. 19(1)(a) is involved.
4. **If refused?** Send it to the **Superintendent of Police** (s. 173(4)), then the **Magistrate under s. 175(3)**, now with an
   **affidavit**.

**Rule of thumb.** Read the punishment band first. 3 to under 7 years → enquiry *possible* (DSP permission, 14 days). 7 years or
more, or under 3 → back to the *Lalita Kumari* default: register.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *Principle:* as in step 3. *Facts:* A complains that B's online post commits an offence punishable
with up to 3 years. The SHO, with the DSP's written permission, starts an enquiry and on day 10 finds no prima facie case. Which is
correct? (A) Illegal: *Lalita Kumari* requires immediate registration. (B) Valid: the offence is in the 3-to-7 band, permission was
taken, and the enquiry ended within 14 days. (C) Illegal: only a Magistrate may hold an enquiry. (D) Valid only if A consented.
→ **(B).** (A) ignores that s. 173(3) now carves an exception into *Lalita Kumari*; (C) and (D) add conditions the principle doesn't
contain.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **"Less than seven."** An offence punishable with 7 years or more is outside s. 173(3).
2. **"May" is not "must".** The enquiry is a power. An option saying the police *must* enquire before every FIR in the band is wrong.
3. **Rank.** Permission must come from an officer **not below DSP** — an Inspector's permission fails.
4. **Old numbers.** s. 154 → 173, s. 156(3) → 175(3), s. 65B → BSA s. 63. CLAT options love the old number.

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 430 words**

For almost fifty years, the first step in most criminal cases in India was governed by section 154 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. When the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita replaced the Code on 1 July 2024, that rule moved to section 173, and it changed in three ways that matter to anyone who walks into a police station.

The first change concerns place. Information about a cognizable offence may now be given at any police station, irrespective of the area where the offence was committed. Courts had long accepted such a "zero FIR", but the new law writes it into the text, and the case is then transferred to the station that has jurisdiction. The second change concerns form. Information may be given orally or by electronic communication; if it is sent electronically, it is taken on record once the informant signs it within three days.

The third change is the one lawyers argue about. In Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar Pradesh (2013), a Constitution Bench held that if information discloses a cognizable offence, the police must register an FIR. A preliminary inquiry was allowed only in narrow categories, such as matrimonial disputes, commercial offences, medical negligence and corruption, and only to find out whether a cognizable offence was disclosed at all. Section 173(3) now lets the officer in charge, with the prior permission of an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, conduct a preliminary enquiry for any offence punishable with three years or more but less than seven years, to decide within fourteen days whether a prima facie case exists. The word the law uses is "may": the enquiry is a power, not a duty.

Critics say this hands the police a filter that Lalita Kumari had taken away, and point out that the fourteen days have no stated starting point. Supporters reply that it shields people from complaints filed only to harass them. In Imran Pratapgadhi v. State of Gujarat (2025), the Supreme Court read the provision protectively: where a complaint about words or art alleges an offence within the three-to-seven-year band, the police should ordinarily use the enquiry to check whether any offence is made out before registering a case, because the freedom of speech in Article 19(1)(a) is at stake.

What has not changed is the remedy when the police refuse. A person whose information is not recorded may still send it to the Superintendent of Police, and then approach a Magistrate, now under section 175(3), which adds a new requirement: the application must be supported by an affidavit.
