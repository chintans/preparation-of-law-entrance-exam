## Lesson: the burden of explaining (≈ 6 min)

**The rule.** **s. 106 Indian Evidence Act 1872**, now **s. 109 Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023**, says that when a fact is *especially within the knowledge* of a person,
the burden of proving that fact is on him. CLAT likes this rule because it looks like it reverses the presumption of innocence. It does not.

**Method: three questions, in this order.**
1. **Has the prosecution done its own work?** Is there evidence which, if believed, would itself sustain a conviction (presence, motive, medical evidence and so on)? If not,
   stop. s. 106 cannot fill the gap. *Shambhu Nath Mehra v. State of Ajmer* (1956) held that the section does not relieve the prosecution of proving guilt beyond
   reasonable doubt. In Nov 2026 the SC restated it (*Kamalbai Waman Patil v. State of Maharashtra*, 5 Nov 2026).
2. **Is this fact really known only to the accused?** It must be pre-eminently or exceptionally within his knowledge, something the prosecution could not prove without
   unreasonable difficulty. Examples: what happened inside a locked house, or where the accused's ticket is.
3. **What did he say?** **Silence** may become an *additional link* in the chain. A **false explanation** is a stronger circumstance (*Ramkishan Digambar Jadhav*, 2 Nov 2026:
   a false "epileptic fit" story completed the chain). Even then, all the circumstances together must point only to guilt.

**Rule of thumb:** *s. 106 completes a chain; it never starts one.* It shifts the burden of **explaining**, never the burden of **proof**.

**Worked example (CLAT style).** *Principle as above. Facts:* a shopkeeper is found dead in his shop. The accused, his tenant, was seen in the market that day, and nothing else
links him. The prosecution asks for a conviction because "he has not explained where he was". **Answer:** no conviction. Being in a public market is not a fact
especially within his knowledge, and the prosecution has led no evidence that would itself sustain guilt. Step 1 fails, so s. 106 never comes in.

**The traps**
- **The "must" option.** "The court must convict because the accused was silent" is wrong. Silence *may* be a link; it never compels a conviction.
- **Proof vs explanation.** "The accused must prove his innocence beyond reasonable doubt" reverses the rule.
- **Lie = conviction.** A false explanation is a circumstance against him, not proof by itself.
- **Old/new numbers.** In the BSA the rule is **s. 109**, not 106 (s. 106 BSA is a different provision). Expect both numbers in options.

## Constitution Capsule — Unit 11: Writs, second pass (≈ 4 min)

**Five flash facts**
1. **Art 32(2)** → the SC may issue directions, orders or writs **"in the nature of"** habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, so it is not limited
   to the English forms. **Art 32(3)**: Parliament may give the same power to other courts. **Art 139**: Parliament may give the SC writ power for purposes beyond Fundamental Rights.
2. **Who may apply.** Normally only an aggrieved person may apply. Three exceptions:
   - **habeas corpus**: anyone may apply on the detenu's behalf;
   - **quo warranto**: **any person** may apply;
   - **PIL** relaxed standing for others (*S. P. Gupta*, 1981; *Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984).
3. **Art 226** → it runs against **"any person or authority"**, including a private body doing a **public duty**. **Art 226(2)** (15th Am. 1963) lets a HC act where the
   cause of action arises wholly or partly in its territory.
4. **Certiorari and prohibition** → aimed at courts, tribunals and authorities that decide rights. They do not lie against a **legislature** or a **private person**.
5. **Art 32 cannot be suspended** except as the Constitution provides (Art 32(4); Art 359). Since the **44th Am.**, Arts **20 and 21** stay enforceable even in an Emergency.

**Say-it drill.** Cover the lines and say what each does: **32(2) · 32(3)/139 · standing exceptions · 226(2) · 32(4)/359**.

**Case:** ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997, 7 judges).** The power of HCs under Arts 226/227 and of the SC under Art 32 is **basic structure**. Tribunal decisions
remain subject to a HC division bench, so tribunals cannot replace the HCs' writ jurisdiction.

**The trap:** "Art 32 can enforce any legal right" is wrong: it covers Fundamental Rights only (Art 226 is wider). "Quo warranto needs a personally aggrieved petitioner" is wrong.
"No writ lies under Art 226 against a private body" is wrong when that body does a public duty. "Tribunals oust HC writ jurisdiction" is wrong (*L. Chandra Kumar*).

Section 106 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, now section 109 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, says that when a fact is especially within the knowledge of any person, the burden of proving that fact is upon him. Read alone, the words seem capable of turning a criminal trial upside down. If a man is found near a body, why should he not be made to explain himself?

The courts have refused that reading for seventy years. In 1956 the Supreme Court held that the section does not relieve the prosecution of its duty to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. It applies only to facts that are pre-eminently or exceptionally within the knowledge of the accused, facts that the prosecution could not prove without unreasonable difficulty. A ticketless traveller can be asked to produce his ticket; he cannot be asked to prove that he did not steal one.

The rule works most often in deaths inside the home. When a wife dies of injuries in a house she shares only with her husband, and he was present that night, how she came by those injuries is something he knows and outsiders do not. If he gives no explanation, the court may treat his silence as an additional link in a chain of circumstances. But it is only an additional link. In November 2026 the Supreme Court restated the point: an accused's failure to explain counts against him only after the prosecution has led evidence which, if believed, would itself sustain a conviction. The section can complete a chain; it cannot start one.

A false explanation stands on a different footing from no explanation. If the husband says that his wife fell down the stairs, and the medical evidence shows that she was strangled, the lie is itself a circumstance pointing towards guilt, because an innocent person has less reason to invent a story. Even then, the court must be satisfied that all the circumstances, taken together, point only to guilt and rule out every reasonable alternative.

The section, in short, shifts the burden of explaining, not the burden of proof. The accused never has to prove his innocence. He has to explain what only he can know, and only once the prosecution has done its own work.
