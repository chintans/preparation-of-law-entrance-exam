# Day 7 — Surprise test, CLAT pattern

**Surprise test** — CLAT pattern, 20 questions, 22 minutes, real marking (+1 / −0.25, unattempted 0). Start the clock now.

Six passages. Read each passage once, then answer. Apply the legal principles exactly as stated.

### Passage I (English) — Q1–4

For most of history, a broken thing was the beginning of a conversation rather than the end of one. A cracked pot
went to the potter, a torn shirt to the tailor, a stalled radio to the man with the soldering iron at the corner of
the market. Repair was not a hobby or a virtue; it was simply what one did, because new things were expensive and the
knowledge of how old things worked was spread widely through a neighbourhood.

That arrangement has quietly come apart. Manufacturers now glue batteries into phones, use screws that need
proprietary drivers, and pair spare parts to individual devices through software, so that a genuine replacement
screen refuses to work until an authorised technician "introduces" it to the handset. The justifications offered are
rarely absurd. Sealed devices resist water; tightly integrated parts make products thinner; software pairing, we are
told, deters theft. Each argument is plausible in isolation. Taken together, however, they produce a world in which
the person who owns an object is the person least permitted to understand it.

The costs are not merely private. A device discarded for want of a ₹600 battery joins the electronic waste that
India, among the world's largest generators of it, struggles to process safely. The small repairer, who once needed
only skill, now needs access — to manuals, to parts, to diagnostic software — that the manufacturer may grant or
withhold at will. When repair becomes a licence rather than a craft, the corner shop does not so much close as become
a franchise.

None of this argues for nostalgia. Few would trade a modern phone for a valve radio, however easy the latter was to
mend. The more modest claim is that durability and repairability are design choices, not laws of nature, and that
markets reward them poorly because their benefits arrive late and are spread thin. A buyer comparing two phones in a
shop sees the price and the camera; she does not see the cost of the battery that will fail in the third year. It is
precisely in such cases — where the information that matters is hidden at the moment of choice — that rules on spare
parts, manuals and fair access do work that individual vigilance cannot.

The right to repair, then, is less a demand to return to the past than a request that ownership mean what it used
to: that the thing in your hand is, in the fullest sense, yours.

**1.** Which of the following best states the author's central claim?
- (A) Modern devices are inferior to older ones and should be redesigned to resemble them.
- (B) Manufacturers' reasons for sealing devices are dishonest and should be disregarded.
- (C) Repairability is a design choice that markets undervalue, which justifies rules guaranteeing access to parts and information.
- (D) Electronic waste is the most serious environmental problem India faces.

**2.** The remark that the corner shop "does not so much close as become a franchise" most strongly suggests that:
- (A) independent repairers survive, but increasingly on terms set by manufacturers who control access.
- (B) small repairers are being driven out entirely by large retail chains.
- (C) franchising has raised the quality of repairs available to consumers.
- (D) consumers now prefer authorised service centres to local shops.

**3.** The author's attitude towards the manufacturers' justifications (water resistance, thinness, theft deterrence) is best described as:
- (A) contemptuous dismissal of each of them.
- (B) wholehearted agreement with all of them.
- (C) indifference, since they are irrelevant to the argument.
- (D) conceding that each has some merit while objecting to their combined effect.

**4.** As used in the passage, "modest" (in "The more modest claim") most nearly means:
- (A) humble in manner.
- (B) limited in scope; less ambitious.
- (C) shy and reserved.
- (D) inexpensive.

### Passage II (Current Affairs) — Q5–9

On 20 November 2025 a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court gave its opinion on a reference made by the
President under **[1]** of the Constitution. The reference, made up of fourteen questions, followed an April 2025
judgment of a two-judge bench in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu*, which had laid down time limits for
Governors and the President to act on Bills and had treated ten long-pending Tamil Nadu Bills as having received
assent. The Constitution Bench, presided over by the then Chief Justice, said that neither Article 200 nor Article 201
fixes a time limit, that courts cannot impose one, and that "deemed assent" has no basis in the Constitution. It added,
however, that prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction by a Governor is open to limited judicial review. Four
days later a new Chief Justice took the oath as the 53rd Chief Justice of India.

**5.** Which of the following correctly fills **[1]**?
- (A) Article 32
- (B) Article 143
- (C) Article 131
- (D) Article 368

**6.** When a Bill passed by a State Legislature is presented to the Governor under Article 200, which of the following is **not** an option open to the Governor?
- (A) Declaring that he assents to the Bill.
- (B) Declaring that he withholds assent.
- (C) Returning a Bill (other than a Money Bill) to the Legislature for reconsideration.
- (D) Referring the Bill directly to the Supreme Court for its opinion on the Bill's validity.

**7.** Who presided over the Constitution Bench that delivered this opinion?
- (A) Justice B. R. Gavai
- (B) Justice D. Y. Chandrachud
- (C) Justice Sanjiv Khanna
- (D) Justice Surya Kant

**8.** Who took the oath as the 53rd Chief Justice of India in November 2025?
- (A) Justice B. R. Gavai
- (B) Justice Vikram Nath
- (C) Justice Surya Kant
- (D) Justice P. S. Narasimha

**9.** Which statement correctly reflects the Constitution Bench's opinion?
- (A) A court may treat a Bill as having received assent if the Governor has not acted on it within three months.
- (B) A court may direct a Governor to take a decision on a Bill after prolonged, unexplained inaction, without examining the merits of the decision.
- (C) A Governor's conduct under Article 200 is wholly immune from judicial scrutiny.
- (D) A Bill re-passed by the Legislature after being returned may again have assent withheld by the Governor.

### Passage III (Legal) — Q10–12

Under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a proposal becomes a promise only when it is accepted, and the acceptance must be
absolute and unqualified. A reply that purports to accept but adds, removes or alters a term is not an acceptance; it
is a counter-proposal, and it operates as a rejection of the original proposal, which the offeree cannot afterwards
revive on his own. Where a proposal prescribes the manner of acceptance and the acceptance is made in some other
manner, the proposer may, within a reasonable time after the acceptance is communicated to him, insist that it be made
in the prescribed manner; if he fails to do so, he accepts the acceptance. Acceptance must also be communicated: a
proposer cannot turn the offeree's silence into consent merely by announcing that silence will be taken as acceptance.

**10.** Meera offers to sell her scooter to Nikhil for ₹70,000. Nikhil replies, "Done at ₹62,000." Meera says no. Two days later Nikhil writes, "All right, ₹70,000 it is — I accept your offer." Meera, who has meanwhile received a better offer, refuses to sell. Decide.
- (A) There is a contract at ₹70,000, because Nikhil finally accepted Meera's own price.
- (B) There is a contract at ₹62,000, because Nikhil's reply came first.
- (C) There is no contract, because Meera received a better offer before Nikhil's second letter.
- (D) There is no contract, because Nikhil's first reply rejected Meera's offer and his second letter was only a fresh proposal Meera could refuse.

**11.** Ritu's written offer to Sameer says, "Reply by registered post only." Sameer accepts by e-mail, which Ritu reads the same day. Ritu says nothing for a month and then claims there is no contract because Sameer did not use registered post. Decide.
- (A) There is a contract, because Ritu did not insist on the prescribed manner within a reasonable time.
- (B) There is no contract, because the prescribed manner was not followed.
- (C) There is no contract, because an e-mail cannot amount to acceptance.
- (D) There is a contract, because the manner of acceptance is never relevant.

**12.** Tarun writes to Uma, "I will buy your three paintings for ₹2 lakh. If I hear nothing from you by the 15th, I will consider them mine." Uma decides to sell, puts the paintings aside and tells her sister so, but never replies. On the 20th she sells them to someone else. Tarun sues Uma for breach. Decide.
- (A) Uma is liable, because she had decided to sell and set the paintings aside.
- (B) Uma is liable, because Tarun fixed the 15th as the deadline and heard nothing.
- (C) Uma is not liable, because she never communicated an acceptance to Tarun.
- (D) Uma is not liable, because she sold the paintings to another buyer.

### Passage IV (Legal) — Q13–14

A person is liable in negligence when (i) he owes the claimant a duty to take reasonable care, (ii) he breaches that
duty by failing to do what a reasonable and prudent person in his position would have done, and (iii) the breach
causes the claimant damage of a kind that was reasonably foreseeable. An occupier of premises open to the public owes
its visitors a duty to take reasonable care that the premises, and any equipment it provides for their use, are
reasonably safe. Where the claimant's own failure to take reasonable care for her safety contributed to the damage, she
does not lose her claim, but her damages are reduced to the extent the court thinks just, having regard to her share
in the responsibility.

**13.** Over a week, three members of FitNest gym told the front desk that the belt of treadmill 4 was frayed and slipping. The manager noted the complaints but neither repaired the machine nor put up a notice. Vani, a member who knew nothing of the complaints, used treadmill 4; the belt tore and she fell, fracturing her wrist. FitNest argues that it never inspected the machine and so did not know it was dangerous. Decide.
- (A) FitNest is not liable, because it had no actual knowledge that the machine was dangerous.
- (B) FitNest is liable, because a reasonable gym would have acted on repeated complaints, and an injury of this kind was foreseeable.
- (C) FitNest is not liable, because Vani chose to use treadmill 4.
- (D) FitNest is liable only if the manager intended Vani to be hurt.

**14.** Assume, in addition, that Vani was running at top speed while typing on her phone with both hands, ignoring a printed instruction on the console to hold the handrail, and that this made her fall worse. Which is the most accurate conclusion?
- (A) FitNest is not liable at all, because Vani was herself careless.
- (B) FitNest is liable in full, because Vani did not know of the complaints.
- (C) FitNest is liable, but Vani's damages may be reduced to reflect her share in the responsibility.
- (D) Vani is liable to FitNest for the damage to the treadmill.

### Passage V (Logical Reasoning) — Q15–18

Over the past three years the city of Varnapur has built 40 kilometres of protected cycle lanes. Over the same period
the number of road accidents involving cyclists in the city has risen by 18 per cent. A group of councillors now argues
that the lanes have made cycling more dangerous, and that the money set aside for a further 30 kilometres should be
spent instead on widening roads for cars. "The numbers speak for themselves," one councillor said. "We built lanes and
got more accidents." Cycling groups reply that the number of people cycling to work in Varnapur has more than doubled
in the same three years, and that most of the accidents occurred at junctions where the protected lanes end and
cyclists must merge with motor traffic.

**15.** Which of the following best describes the main flaw in the councillors' reasoning?
- (A) It assumes that because accidents rose after the lanes were built, the lanes caused the rise, ignoring the change in the number of people cycling.
- (B) It relies on a survey of cyclists' opinions rather than on accident data.
- (C) It attacks the motives of the cycling groups instead of their argument.
- (D) It treats the view of a single councillor as the view of the whole council.

**16.** Which of the following, if true, most strengthens the cycling groups' position?
- (A) Several cyclists say they feel safer in the protected lanes.
- (B) Car traffic in Varnapur rose by 25 per cent over the same three years.
- (C) Most of the cyclists involved in accidents were not wearing helmets.
- (D) Accidents per thousand cycling trips in Varnapur fell by about a third over the three years.

**17.** If the cycling groups' account of where the accidents occurred is correct, which course of action does it most directly support?
- (A) Removing the existing protected lanes.
- (B) Redesigning junctions so that protected lanes continue through them rather than ending before them.
- (C) Banning cyclists from roads that have junctions.
- (D) Spending the remaining money on widening roads for cars.

**18.** Which of the following arguments is most similar in its reasoning to the councillors' argument?
- (A) Borrowing at the library doubled after it extended its opening hours, and most of the extra loans were made during the new evening hours; so the longer hours increased borrowing.
- (B) Most students who take coaching pass the exam; so coaching guarantees success.
- (C) Since the hospital opened a new cardiac ward, more heart-attack deaths have been recorded at the hospital; so the ward is harming patients.
- (D) The new highway has fewer accidents than the old road; so the old road should be closed.

### Passage VI (Quantitative Techniques) — Q19–20

A coaching institute ran a full-length CLAT mock (120 questions; +1 for a correct answer, −0.25 for a wrong one, 0 for
an unattempted one) for 800 candidates. 60% of the candidates attempted all 120 questions; of these, 25% scored above
90. Of the candidates who left at least one question unattempted, 15% scored above 90.

**19.** How many candidates scored above 90?
- (A) 144
- (B) 160
- (C) 192
- (D) 168

**20.** A candidate attempted 104 questions and got 86 of them right. What is the candidate's score?
- (A) 81.5
- (B) 86
- (C) 77.5
- (D) 82.5
