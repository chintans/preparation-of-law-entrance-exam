## Lesson — Legal current affairs: read a judgment report like an examiner (≈ 8 min)

**Why this.** LEG-NEW is a weight-3 (core) topic for NLSAT and CLAT, and you are 0/2 on it. One of those misses (Day 7) was
the classic error: you took what an *earlier* bench had said ("deemed assent") as what the Constitution Bench finally *held*.
NLSAT and CLAT give a 350–450-word news-style report on a judgment and ask about five predictable things in it.

**The method — FIHRR + Hook.** As you read, tag each sentence with one letter:

| Tag | Question | Where it hides |
|---|---|---|
| **F**acts | Who did what to whom? | opening paragraph |
| **I**ssue | What legal question did the Court have to answer? | "The argument rested on…", "the question was…" |
| **H**olding | What did *this* Court decide on that question? | "The Supreme Court held / disagreed / ruled…" |
| **R**eason | Why — what test or distinction? | "because…", "the test is…" |
| **R**esult | What order was actually made? | last paragraph: *set aside, remitted, upheld, quashed, dismissed* |
| **Hook** | Which Article / section / body sits underneath? | named once, often in passing |

**Rule of thumb.** In any report, find the *verb attached to the final court* (held, set aside, dismissed, remitted). Everything
said by a lower court, a party or a counsel is a **candidate distractor**, not the answer.

**Worked example (NLSAT style).** "In November 2025 a five-judge bench answered the President's reference on Governors and
Bills. Which of these did the Court hold? (A) Bills pending too long are deemed to have received assent (B) Courts cannot fix
timelines for Governors, but may direct a decision in cases of prolonged, unexplained inaction (C) The Governor may withhold a
Bill indefinitely (D) The reference was made under Article 200."
F: President asked; I: can courts fix timelines / deem assent; **H: no timelines, no deemed assent, limited review of inaction**;
Hook: Art. 143. → **(B)**. (A) is what the *April 2025 two-judge* ruling did — a lower/earlier holding, the classic distractor.
(D) is the subject Article, not the route.

**The traps examiners set.**
1. **Lower court as answer.** The High Court's view appears in the passage in confident language; the question asks what the
   Supreme Court held.
2. **Result inflation.** "Set aside and remitted" ≠ "convicted" or "acquitted". A remand decides only the point argued.
3. **Near-synonyms.** *Public servant / government servant / "State"*, *void / voidable*, *stay / quash*: the case is usually
   about the difference.
4. **Old vs new code numbers.** Expect "now section __ of the BNS/BNSS/BSA" questions. Today's anchor: **CrPC 197 → BNSS 218**.

**Passage (Questions 1–5) — 449 words**

Three engineers of a power distribution company owned by the Rajasthan government — an executive engineer, an assistant engineer and a junior engineer — were accused of helping a steel firm tamper with its electricity meters. The loss alleged ran to about ₹13 crore, much of it never paid. The police filed a chargesheet and the trial court took cognizance. The engineers then went to the High Court with a short argument: no court could proceed against them at all, because the government had never granted sanction to prosecute them.

The argument rested on Section 197 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, now Section 218 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita. The provision shields certain officials from prosecution for acts done in the discharge of official duty unless the government first gives its approval. Its purpose is to protect honest officers from vexatious cases, so that the fear of litigation does not paralyse administration. But the shield is narrow by design. It covers only a public servant who is not removable from office except by or with the sanction of the government.

The High Court accepted the plea and set aside the cognizance order. The engineers were, after all, public servants: Section 169 of the Electricity Act, 2003 says so. The Supreme Court, in a judgment by Justices Prashant Kumar Mishra and Shree Chandrashekhar reported on 3 November 2026, disagreed. Being a public servant, the Court said, does not by itself make an employee a government servant. These engineers had been appointed by the company, not by the State government, and the State government had no power to dismiss them. The test in Section 197 asks who controls removal, and here the company, not the government, held that power.

Nor did it help that the company is "State" under Article 12 of the Constitution. That label answers a different question — whether a body must respect fundamental rights and can be called to account through a writ. It does not turn every employee of a public undertaking into an officer of the government. The Court relied on earlier rulings to the same effect, among them Mohd. Hadi Raja v. State of Bihar (1998).

The Supreme Court set aside the High Court's orders and sent the engineers' revision petitions back to it, to be decided on their merits — including whether the material in the chargesheet was enough to frame charges. The engineers have not been convicted of anything; they have only lost a procedural shield they were never entitled to. For a student of law, the case is a reminder that three phrases often used as if they meant the same thing — public servant, government servant and "State" — do three quite different jobs.
