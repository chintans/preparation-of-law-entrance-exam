# Day 9 — Contract, part 3: who can contract, and what makes a promise worth enforcing (CLAT)

## Lesson

**Why this, today.** Offer–acceptance timing has had three lessons already. CLAT's contract passages lean just as
hard on two other ideas: **capacity** (above all, minors) and **consideration**. Both have a handful of fixed rules
and one or two famous cases that passages are built around.

### 1. Minors — the agreement is *void*, not voidable
- s. 11: competent to contract = a major (18+), of sound mind, not disqualified by law.
- *Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose* (Privy Council, 1903): a minor's agreement is **void ab initio**. The minor had
  mortgaged his house for a loan; the lender could neither enforce the mortgage nor recover the money advanced.
- Consequences to remember:
  1. The minor cannot be sued on the promise — **not even after turning 18**.
  2. **No ratification** on majority: you can't breathe life into something that never existed. A fresh contract,
     with **fresh consideration**, is needed.
  3. **Necessaries (s. 68):** a supplier of necessaries *suited to the minor's condition in life* is reimbursed
     **out of the minor's property** — the minor is **not personally liable**. Goods are not "necessaries" if the
     minor is already adequately supplied with them (English authority: *Nash v Inman*, 1908).

### 2. Consideration — s. 2(d) in one line
"**At the desire of the promisor**, the promisee **or any other person** has done / does / promises to do (or abstain
from) something." Two exam hooks:
- **At whose desire?** An act done at someone *else's* request is no consideration for the promisor's later promise.
  *Durga Prasad v Baldeo* (1880): shops built at the Collector's request were no consideration for traders' later
  promise of commission.
- **From whom?** Consideration may move from a **third person** (unlike English law). *Chinnaya v Ramayya* (1882):
  a mother gifted property to her daughter on condition the daughter pay an annuity to the mother's brother; the
  daughter's promise to him was enforceable — the mother's gift was the consideration.

**s. 25 — three exceptions where no consideration is needed:** (1) written **and registered**, out of natural love
and affection between near relations; (2) promise to compensate someone who has **already voluntarily done something
for the promisor**; (3) written, signed promise to pay a **time-barred debt**.

### Method
Ask in order: **Can this person contract?** → **Was the act done at *this* promisor's desire?** → **Does an s. 25
exception rescue it?** Most wrong options skip a step.

### Worked example (CLAT style)
*Facts:* Ishaan, 17, borrows ₹50,000 from Rahul to pay his coaching fees. At 18 he signs a note: "I confirm the loan
and will repay it by March." Rahul sues on the note.
(A) Rahul succeeds — Ishaan confirmed the debt as an adult. (B) Rahul succeeds — coaching fees are necessaries.
(C) Rahul fails — the loan agreement was void and a confirmation without fresh consideration cannot revive it.
(D) Rahul fails — Rahul should have checked Ishaan's age.
**Answer: (C).** (A) is the ratification trap. (B) is tempting, but s. 68 covers the *supplier* of necessaries, and
even then only reimbursement from property — there is no personal liability on a note. (D) is a moral reason, not
the rule.

**The trap examiners set.** Treating a minor's agreement as *voidable* ("he can confirm it later") and treating any
earlier act as consideration without asking *at whose desire* it was done.

### Passage I — Q1–4

The law of contract assumes a bargain between people able to look after their own interests. For that reason it
draws a hard line around those who, in its view, cannot. Under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, every person is
competent to contract who has attained the age of majority, is of sound mind and is not disqualified by any law. The
courts have read this strictly. An agreement with a minor is not merely open to be set aside at the minor's option;
it is void from the very beginning. It follows that the minor cannot be made to perform it, either while a minor or
after attaining majority, and that a promise made after majority to honour such an agreement does not bind him unless
it is itself a fresh contract supported by fresh consideration.

The law does not, however, leave those who deal with minors entirely without remedy. Where a person incapable of
entering into a contract is supplied by another with necessaries suited to his condition in life, the person who
furnished the supplies is entitled to be reimbursed from the property of the incapable person. This creates no
personal liability; the supplier looks only to the minor's property. Goods are not necessaries if the minor is
already adequately supplied with goods of that kind, however suitable they may otherwise be to his station.

Critics argue that the rule rewards the dishonest teenager who lies about his age. Its defenders reply that a rule
which bends whenever a minor appears worldly ceases to protect the minors who are not.

**1.** Aryan, aged 17, borrows ₹2 lakh from Mr Kapoor to buy a gaming console and signs a promissory note for the
amount. When Aryan turns 18, Kapoor sues him on the note. Which is correct?
- (A) Kapoor succeeds, because Aryan is now a major and can be held to his promise.
- (B) Kapoor succeeds, because a promissory note is a written acknowledgment of the debt.
- (C) Kapoor fails, because the agreement was void from the beginning and Aryan's attaining majority does not change that.
- (D) Kapoor fails, because he did not verify Aryan's age before lending.

**2.** On his 18th birthday Aryan writes to Kapoor: "I hereby ratify my promise to repay the ₹2 lakh." Kapoor gives
nothing new in return. Kapoor sues on the letter. Which is correct?
- (A) Kapoor fails, because a void agreement cannot be ratified and the letter is not supported by fresh consideration.
- (B) Kapoor succeeds, because ratification relates back and makes the original loan valid from the start.
- (C) Kapoor succeeds, because the letter is a fresh written contract made by a major.
- (D) Kapoor fails, because a ratification is valid only if it is registered.

**3.** Tanvi, 16, lives in a school hostel after her parents' death and owns a flat she inherited. She has no winter
uniform. A tailor stitches her the school's prescribed winter uniform for ₹8,000 on credit. She does not pay. Which
is correct?
- (A) The tailor can recover ₹8,000 from Tanvi personally, because the uniform was a necessary.
- (B) The tailor is entitled to be reimbursed out of Tanvi's property, but Tanvi is not personally liable.
- (C) The tailor can recover nothing, because an agreement with a minor is void.
- (D) The tailor can recover only if Tanvi confirms the debt after she turns 18.

**4.** Rohan, 17, is the son of a wealthy industrialist, owns inherited shares, and already has a large wardrobe of
designer clothes. He buys eleven designer jackets on credit from a boutique. The boutique claims reimbursement out of
his shares, arguing that designer jackets are suited to a boy of his wealth. Which is correct?
- (A) The boutique succeeds, because the jackets are suited to Rohan's condition in life.
- (B) The boutique succeeds, but only for the value of one jacket.
- (C) The boutique fails, because no supply to a minor can ever be reimbursed.
- (D) The boutique fails, because Rohan was already adequately supplied with such goods, so they are not necessaries, however suited to his station.

### Passage II — Q5–6

A promise is enforced not because it was made but because something was given for it. Under the Indian Contract Act,
when, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or
abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act, abstinence or promise is called
a consideration for the promise. Two features distinguish this definition. First, the act must be done at the desire
of the promisor, not of someone else. Secondly, consideration need not move from the promisee; it may move from any
other person.

An agreement made without consideration is void, unless (i) it is in writing and registered and is made on account
of natural love and affection between parties standing in a near relation to each other; or (ii) it is a promise to
compensate, wholly or in part, a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor; or (iii) it is
a written, signed promise to pay a debt barred by limitation.

**5.** At the request of the Municipal Commissioner, Sameer builds a row of shops in a new market at his own expense.
Traders later occupy the shops and, out of gratitude, promise Sameer a commission on their sales "in consideration of
the expense he incurred". They never pay. Is the traders' promise enforceable?
- (A) No, because the shops were built at the desire of the Commissioner, not the traders, and were not done voluntarily for the traders.
- (B) Yes, because the traders benefited from the shops Sameer built.
- (C) Yes, because past consideration is valid consideration under Indian law.
- (D) No, because a promise made out of gratitude can never be enforced.

**6.** Savitri gifts her house to her daughter Lakshmi on the condition that Lakshmi pays ₹1 lakh a year to Savitri's
brother Gopal. The same day Lakshmi promises Gopal in writing that she will pay him. After two years she stops,
arguing that Gopal gave her nothing. Which is correct?
- (A) Gopal fails, because consideration did not move from him.
- (B) Gopal succeeds, because consideration may move from any other person, and Savitri's gift is consideration for Lakshmi's promise.
- (C) Gopal succeeds only because Lakshmi and Gopal are near relatives acting out of natural love and affection.
- (D) Gopal fails, because the promise to him was not registered.

### Passage III — Q7

Under the Indian Contract Act, the communication of an acceptance is complete as against the proposer when it is put
in a course of transmission to him so as to be out of the power of the acceptor. A proposal may be revoked at any time
before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards. The communication
of a revocation is complete, as against the person to whom it is made, when it comes to his knowledge.

**7.** On 2 November Leela emails Karan an offer to sell her scooter, which he reads the same day. On 8 November Leela
posts a letter revoking the offer; it reaches Karan on 11 November. On 10 November Karan posts his acceptance, which
reaches Leela on 12 November. Which is correct?
- (A) There is no contract, because Leela posted her revocation before Karan posted his acceptance.
- (B) There is no contract, because Leela's revocation reached Karan before his acceptance reached Leela.
- (C) There is a contract, but it is formed only on 12 November.
- (D) There is a contract, because the acceptance was complete against Leela when posted on 10 November, before the revocation came to Karan's knowledge on 11 November.

### Passage IV — Q8

Every person has a right to defend his own body against any offence affecting the human body. The right begins as
soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt or threat to commit the offence, and
continues only as long as that apprehension continues. It never extends to causing more harm than is necessary for
the purpose of defence.

**8.** A burglar enters Pooja's flat at night and comes at her with a knife. She strikes his arm with a heavy vase; he
drops the knife and runs out of the flat and down the stairs. Pooja chases him into the street and hits him on the
back with a cricket bat, fracturing a rib. Which is correct?
- (A) Pooja is protected for both blows, because the burglar attacked her with a knife inside her own home.
- (B) Pooja is protected for neither blow, because she could have locked herself in a room.
- (C) Pooja is protected for the vase blow but not for the bat blow, because the apprehension of danger ended when the burglar dropped the knife and fled.
- (D) Pooja is not protected for the vase blow, because a heavy vase is a dangerous weapon.
